HW4 · How often do climate stories hedge on the science?

Out Mon Nov 2 · due Mon Nov 9, 11:59 pm

AI assistance is recommended on this one. Use it, disclose it, and write the note yourself.

The question

Boykoff & Boykoff (GLOBAL ENVIRONMENTAL CHANGE, 2004) read the climate coverage of four US newspapers from 1988 to 2002 by hand. About half the stories gave equal space to the scientists who said people are warming the planet and to those who doubted it. How often did New York Times climate stories state warming, or its human cause, as uncertain from 1988 to 2026? Here you will use a large language model with open weights on your own laptop to read these stories and make a similar classification.

What counts as uncertain

Only the warming itself, or people’s role in causing it, counts. Some made-up sentences and how to label them:

Sentence Label
Scientists predict that burning coal and oil may warm the planet. uncertain
Whether people are changing the climate is still in dispute. uncertain
The planet has warmed about 1 degree Celsius in a century, mostly from burning fossil fuels. fact
Warming could cut wheat yields by a tenth, though estimates vary. fact: the doubt is about an effect
Some senators doubt that people cause warming; most climate scientists say the evidence is clear. fact: the story reports the doubt and states the science as settled
Delegates argued late into the night over emission targets. not stated

Target image

Data

  1. The New York Times: an API. The Archive API returns everything the paper published in one month as JSON: headline, summary, first paragraph, date, type, subject tags and address. There is no article text. https://api.nytimes.com/svc/archive/v1/1988/6.json?api-key=... It needs a free key: create an account at https://developer.nytimes.com, add an app, and enable the Archive API for it. The API allows 500 requests a day, 12 seconds apart, so the 466 months from January 1988 to October 2026 take about two and a half hours.
  2. Global temperature: a CSV. NASA GISTEMP v4, one row a year, the annual mean in column J-D: https://data.giss.nasa.gov/gistemp/tabledata_v4/GLB.Ts+dSST.csv
  3. The model: open weights, on your laptop. qwen3.5:9b-q4_K_M, run through Ollama. Setup is below.

A month holds 4,000 to 10,000 items. Keep the climate items and the fields you use, save each month, and never request a month twice.

How long it took me

On my laptop (a MacBook Pro with an M2 Max chip and 64 GB of memory), the 466 downloads took about two and a half hours and the model close to three hours: about 2,900 topic calls and 2,000 hedging calls, close to 2 seconds each. A laptop without an Apple chip or a graphics card can be several times slower. Time the model on 20 stories, then plan the full run, overnight if you need to. Save every reply, so a run that stops picks up where it stopped. Start early.

The model

  1. Install Ollama from https://ollama.com/download (Mac, Windows or Linux) and open it. It runs in the background and serves the model on your laptop.
  2. In the Terminal, download the model once (6.6 GB): ollama pull qwen3.5:9b-q4_K_M. On a laptop with 8 GB of memory or less, use qwen3.5:4b-q4_K_M (3.3 GB) instead and say so in NOTES.md.
  3. Check that it answers: ollama run qwen3.5:9b-q4_K_M "Say hello".
  4. In R, install ellmer and call the model with chat_ollama(). In params(), set the temperature to 0, the seed to 6850, and reasoning_effort = "none" to turn its thinking off. With thinking on, each call takes much longer.

Your key

Save it in ~/.Renviron as NYT_KEY=..., restart R, and read it with Sys.getenv("NYT_KEY"). The key must never appear in your code, your notes or a commit. GitHub keeps every commit, so a key deleted later is still in the history; if one reaches GitHub, delete it at developer.nytimes.com and make a new one.

Which stories

  • Tagged stories carry one of the paper’s subject tags: Global Warming, Greenhouse Effect, Greenhouse Gas Emissions, or United Nations Framework Convention on Climate Change. The tags are in keywords, where name is subject; older items have them in capitals.
  • News stories only: type_of_material is News. Drop blog posts, whose address contains blogs.nytimes.com or /blogs/ (some years type them as News), and stories that appear twice.
  • At most 100 stories a year, drawn at random from that year’s tagged stories after set.seed(6850), so the model’s work stays near three hours. Every year before 2006 has fewer than 100, so all of them are read. That is about 2,900 of the 10,275 stories. You may have the model read every story; on a laptop that takes about three times as long.

The labels

Ask the model two questions about each story’s headline, summary and first paragraph, one call each, without the date:

  1. Is the story’s main subject climate change? This is a second filter: the tags also catch stories that only touch on climate.
  2. For the stories that are: does it state that the Earth is warming, or that people are causing it, as fact or as uncertain, or does it say nothing about it? Only the warming and its cause count. Doubt about the effects, the costs or a treaty does not.

Asking the model to copy the words about warming before it answers helps. Write each prompt like the codebook in session 17: one sentence on what the model reads, one line defining each label, with the words that signal uncertainty (predict, believe, may, could, theory), then rules for the cases in between, including what does not count. My climate-filter prompt has 3 sentences (about 75 words) and my hedging prompt 10 (about 180). Label at least 30 stories yourself before you look at the model’s answers, and compare. Change one thing in the prompt at a time, and check each version against your hand labels.

Output

  • figures/hw4_target.png, 10 × 10.5 inches at 200 dpi.
  • data_clean/labels.csv: one row per story the model read, with its id, date and both answers.
  • data_clean/stories_per_year.csv: the tagged news stories each year.
  • NOTES.md: the number of stories left after each filter, your two questions word for word, your hand check (how many stories, how many the model got right, and the ones it got wrong), and an AI disclosure (tools, uses, what you checked).

Figure rules

  • Three panels stacked on one time axis, 1988 to 2026. Mark 2026 with a star: it runs to October.
  • Top: climate stories per year, the tagged news stories times the share the model found to be about climate change. An arrow and a label mark five events: the UN climate conferences in Kyoto (COP3, 1997), Copenhagen (COP15, 2009), Paris (COP21, 2015) and Glasgow (COP26, 2021), and the IPCC’s Fourth Assessment Report (AR4, 2007).
  • Middle: each year’s share of climate stories that state warming, or its human cause, as uncertain, one bar a year.
  • Bottom: one cell a year, coloured by global temperature from cool to warm.
  • A dark background.

Font, exact shades and the key’s layout are never differences. Your bars may not match the target image exactly: the stories in your sample, the wording of your two questions and the model you use all move them a little.

How to submit

Your repository is hw4-<your github username> in the course organization. It holds only a README that links to this page; the project’s layout is yours to set up. Put the code in code/, with code/run_everything.R running it all, and paths relative to the project root. Do not commit the months you download, since the paper’s terms do not allow sharing them: list that folder in .gitignore. Commit the two CSV files above instead. The grader has no NYT key, so the script that draws the figure must run from a fresh clone with those two files and NASA’s CSV. Commit as you go; the grader uses the last commit pushed before the deadline.
