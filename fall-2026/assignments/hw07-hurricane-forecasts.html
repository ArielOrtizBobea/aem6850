HW7 · Have hurricane forecasts become more accurate?

Out Thu Nov 26 · due Sun Dec 6, 11:59 pm

AI assistance is recommended on this one. Use it, disclose it, and write the note yourself.

The question

Hurricane Katrina reached Louisiana on August 29, 2005. It killed 1,392 people and caused 125 billion dollars of damage. Sixteen years later to the day, Hurricane Ida reached the same coast with winds of 240 km/h.

The National Hurricane Center forecasts every Atlantic storm every six hours. A forecast says where the centre of the storm will be and how strong its wind will be, up to five days ahead. Around the forecast track the Center draws a cone. The cone is as wide as the Center’s own errors of the five years before, so the storm stays inside it about two times out of three.

People act on these forecasts. They leave, protect their homes and move goods, and a wrong forecast costs money and lives. Martinez (Econometrics, 2020) finds that hurricanes whose landfall was forecast with a larger error caused more damage. Molina & Rudik (NBER Working Paper 32548, 2024) find that better forecasts since 2007 cut the cost of a hurricane by 23%, about 2 billion dollars a storm.

Have the forecasts become more accurate? In this homework you measure the error of every official forecast from 1970 to 2025. Then you compare two forecasts made on August 26, three days before the storm reached Louisiana: Katrina’s in 2005 and Ida’s in 2021.

This is a spatial problem: distances between points, a polygon built from circles, a point inside a polygon, and a grid summed inside a polygon.

Target image

Data

All of it is public and needs no key.

  1. Forecasts, from the National Hurricane Center (one file per storm). A file holds every forecast made for a storm, by the Center and by each computer model. Read the notes on the archive and what each field of a line is. There is one folder per year, from 1970 to 2025. The Atlantic files start with aal. The second address is Katrina, storm 12 of 2005, which the best track calls AL122005.

    https://ftp.nhc.noaa.gov/atcf/archive/2005/
https://ftp.nhc.noaa.gov/atcf/archive/2005/aal122005.dat.gz

    Keep the lines of the technique OFCL, the Center’s own forecast. A file repeats a forecast on several lines: keep one line for each storm, start of the forecast and hours ahead. A latitude of 0 means no position was forecast, and a wind of 0 means no wind was forecast.

  2. Best track, from the National Hurricane Center (one text file). The Center’s final record of each storm: its position, its status and its maximum sustained wind every six hours. Read how the file is laid out.

    https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/data/hurdat/hurdat2-1851-2025-092326.txt

  3. A cone the Center published (a shapefile in a zip). The cone of Hurricane Milton, advisory 12, made public on October 7, 2024 at 10 pm CDT. The cone is the file al142024-012_5day_pgn.shp. Read how the Center defines the cone.

    https://www.nhc.noaa.gov/gis/forecast/archive/al142024_5day_012.zip

  4. Population, from the Global Human Settlement Layer (tiles). The number of people in each cell of 30 arc-seconds, about 1 km, every five years (description). A tile covers 10 by 10 degrees. You need rows 6 and 7 and columns 9 to 11 of 2005, and rows 6 to 8 and the same columns of 2020. This is row 7, column 10 of 2020; for 2005, write E2005 in place of E2020 in both places. The server is slow: raise R’s time limit for a download with options(timeout = 900).

    https://jeodpp.jrc.ec.europa.eu/ftp/jrc-opendata/GHSL/GHS_POP_GLOBE_R2023A/GHS_POP_E2020_GLOBE_R2023A_4326_30ss/V1-0/tiles/GHS_POP_E2020_GLOBE_R2023A_4326_30ss_V1_0_R7_C10.zip

  5. Map layers.

    The states, from the Census Bureau (description):

    https://www2.census.gov/geo/tiger/GENZ2023/shp/cb_2023_us_state_20m.zip

    The land, from Natural Earth (description):

    https://naciscdn.org/naturalearth/50m/physical/ne_50m_land.zip

The errors

  • Compare each forecast position with the best track at the time the forecast was for: its start plus its hours. A forecast belongs to the year it starts in.
  • The distance error is the distance between the forecast position and the actual one, in km, along the Earth’s surface (st_distance() on longitude and latitude).
  • The wind speed error is the difference between the forecast wind and the actual one, without its sign, in km/h. A knot is 1.852 km/h. The archive has wind forecasts from 1990.
  • Count a forecast when the best track calls the storm TS, SS or HU both at the start of the forecast and at the time it was for.
  • Panels C and D show a five-year average at 24, 48 and 72 hours: for each year, the average over all the forecasts of that year, the two years before and the two years after. At both ends of the series, use the years there are.
  • For the forecasts of 2005 alone, my average distance error at 72 hours is 278.0 km, over 242 forecasts. For 2021 alone it is 113.8 km, over 138 forecasts.

The cones

Build each cone yourself. The Center’s archive of cones starts in 2008, after Katrina.

  • Radius. For a storm of year y, take the forecasts of the five years before y. The radius at a lead time is the distance error that two-thirds of them stayed under (the 2/3 quantile). For the radii, also count the forecasts where the storm was a depression, TD or SD, at either time. The radius at the start of a forecast is 30 km (16 nautical miles).
  • Shape. Take the forecast positions at 0, 12, 24, 36, 48 and 72 hours. Fill in every hour between them: the position along a smooth curve through the six positions, the radius along a straight line between lead times. Draw a circle each hour. The cone is the area the circles sweep. Work on a flat map in metres centred on the forecast. I used a natural spline and an azimuthal equidistant projection.
  • Check. Build the cone of Milton’s forecast that starts on October 8, 2024 at 00:00 UTC (storm 14 of 2024), with the positions at 96 and 120 hours added, and compare it with the Center’s shapefile. The overlap is the area both cones cover, divided by the area either covers. It must be above 95%. Mine is 98.6%.
  • My radii at 24, 48 and 72 hours are 144, 267 and 386 km for 2005, and 73, 127 and 189 km for 2021.

The two forecasts

  • Katrina, storm 12 of 2005: the forecast that starts on August 26 at 12:00 UTC.
  • Ida, storm 9 of 2021: the forecast that starts on August 26 at 18:00 UTC.
  • For each, after 24, 48 and 72 hours: the distance error, the wind speed error, and whether the storm was inside the cone.
  • For each, the US residents inside the cone: the sum of the population grid over the part of the cone inside the lower 48 states of the Census file (all states but Alaska, Hawaii and Puerto Rico). Use the grid of 2005 for Katrina and of 2020 for Ida.

What the figure says

After three days, Ida’s forecast was 33 km off and Katrina’s 319 km. Over the three days, Ida’s wind speed errors were as large as Katrina’s. In NOTES.md, say in two or three sentences what panels C and D show about each error since 1990.

Figure rules

  • The figure has one title, a dark background and one line of sources at the bottom. Titles, labels and colours are those of the target.
  • Panels A and B, the same area at the same scale: longitude -96.5 to -76, latitude 15.8 to 34.4. Behind each map, the people per km² in 2020 in four classes (from 1, 10, 100 and 1,000) and the sea in blue. For the density, add up the people of 3 by 3 cells and divide by their area. On each map: the cone and the forecast track to 72 hours, the actual track from the start of the forecast to 78 hours later, and a bar from the forecast position to the actual one after 1, 2 and 3 days. The days, the start of the forecast, the landfall in Louisiana and New Orleans are labelled. Map A has two keys and both maps have a scale.
  • Under each map, a table: the distance error and the wind speed error after 1, 2 and 3 days, and the US residents inside the cone.
  • Panels C and D, one line per lead time, labelled at its end, with the two forecasts marked. Panel D has the years of panel C: its lines start in 1990 and the years before are shaded.
  • C sits under A and D under B, with the same left and right edges.

Commits

Commit each step when it works. A healthy history for a project like this has a series of commits. For example:

  1. The project organized: folders, .gitignore, code/run_everything.R.
  2. The code that gets the data: forecasts and best track.
  3. The code that measures the errors.
  4. The cones and the check against the Center’s.
  5. The residents inside each cone.
  6. Panels A and B, with their tables.
  7. Panels C and D.
  8. A last pass that cleans up the code.

This order is just an example. You can adopt your own workflow.

How to submit

Your repository is hw7-<your github username> in the course organization. Put the code in code/, with code/run_everything.R running it all, and paths relative to the project root. Push the code, the two tables, the figure, NOTES.md, and the files your figure script reads (the cones, the tracks and the map layers, under 25 MB in all). The grader downloads nothing, so the script that draws the figure must run from a fresh clone. The grader uses the last commit pushed before the deadline.
