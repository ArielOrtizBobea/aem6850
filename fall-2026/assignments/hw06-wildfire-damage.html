HW6 · Can a model trained on one wildfire predict which homes another destroyed?

Out Fri Nov 13 · due Tue Nov 24, 11:59 pm

AI assistance is recommended on this one. Use it, disclose it, and write the note yourself.

The question

In January 2025 two wildfires burned in Los Angeles County at the same time. The Palisades fire was on the coast. The Eaton fire was in Altadena, about 35 km inland. Together they killed 31 people and destroyed more than 16,000 buildings.

Economists study the various impacts of disasters like this. Their questions often start from one fact about each property: was it destroyed? Hornbeck & Keniston (American Economic Review, 2017) compare the plots of land that burned in Boston’s Great Fire of 1872 with those that did not. Fu & Gregory (Econometrica, 2019) study which damaged homes were rebuilt in New Orleans after Hurricane Katrina. Gallagher & Hartley (American Economic Journal: Economic Policy, 2017) follow the debts of New Orleans residents by how badly their area was flooded.

After a wildfire, inspectors walk to every building and write down how damaged it is. That takes many people and a lot of time. A plane is faster: NOAA photographed both burned areas from the air in one day, on 28 January.

Can a model look at the photo of a home and say whether the home was destroyed? In this homework you train a model on the homes of the Palisades fire, where you know what the inspectors wrote. You use it to predict which homes the Eaton fire destroyed. Then you check your predictions against what the inspectors wrote in Eaton. You do this three times, with photos 20, 40 and 60 m wide around each home.

This is a classification: the model puts each home in one of two groups, destroyed or undamaged.

Target image

Data

All of it is public and needs no key. Each source has a page that describes it, linked below.

  1. Inspections, from CAL FIRE (an ArcGIS server). One point per building, with its type and its damage. Read what the inspections record. There is one layer per fire, described here for Palisades and for Eaton.

    https://services1.arcgis.com/jUJYIo9tSA7EHvfZ/arcgis/rest/services/DINS_2025_Palisades_Public_View/FeatureServer/0
https://services1.arcgis.com/jUJYIo9tSA7EHvfZ/arcgis/rest/services/DINS_2025_Eaton_Public_View/FeatureServer/0

    Add this to either address. The server sends the points in pages (resultOffset, resultRecordCount).

    /query?where=1=1&outFields=OBJECTID,DAMAGE,STRUCTURETYPE&outSR=4326&orderByFields=OBJECTID&f=json

  2. Photos, from NOAA (map tiles). The flight of 28 January 2025. Read about NOAA’s emergency photos and look at these photos on a map.

    https://stormscdn.ngs.noaa.gov/20250128a-rgb/{zoom}/{column}/{row}

    This is the address scheme of any web map. A tile is 256 × 256 pixels. On the ground, a pixel is about 12 cm at zoom 20 and 25 cm at zoom 19. Those sizes include the latitude: a web map’s pixel covers 15 cm at zoom 20 on the equator, and that size times the cosine of the latitude (0.83 here) anywhere else. The address has no extension: a tile is a JPEG or a PNG, and a PNG of 1 × 1 pixel where the flight has no photo.

  3. Map layers.

    Elevation, as GeoTIFF tiles (description). Zoom 12 is enough for the map:

    https://s3.amazonaws.com/elevation-tiles-prod/geotiff/{zoom}/{column}/{row}.tif

    Fire perimeters, from CAL FIRE (description):

    https://services1.arcgis.com/jUJYIo9tSA7EHvfZ/arcgis/rest/services/2025_California_Fire_Perimeters_View/FeatureServer/0

    Roads, from the Census Bureau, layers 2, 6 and 8 (description):

    https://tigerweb.geo.census.gov/arcgis/rest/services/TIGERweb/Transportation/MapServer

    Parcels, from the Los Angeles County Assessor (description). The server sends them in pages too:

    https://services.arcgis.com/RmCCgQtiZLDCtblq/arcgis/rest/services/2025_Wild_Fire_Parcels_100M_Plus_DINS_MaxMin_DAMAGE_VIEW/FeatureServer/0

Downloading the photos

  • NOAA’s photo of this flight is huge. It spans 73 km from west to east and 24 km from south to north. At zoom 20, that is about 590,000 by 190,000 pixels. NOAA’s own download of the whole photo is one file of 28 GB.
  • You need a small part of it: the squares around the homes. So download tiles, and only the tiles your views touch: 34,032 tiles, 1.1 GB in all. curl::multi_download() fetches many tiles at a time.
  • Save every tile and never request one twice. Your script must be able to stop and start again where it left off.
  • Build every step on 100 homes first, then run all of them.
  • Tiles, views and the model’s numbers stay out of your repository: list their folders in .gitignore before your first commit. You need about 3 GB of disk.

Homes and views

  • Keep the single-family residences (STRUCTURETYPE starts with “Single Family Residence”) recorded as Destroyed (>50%) or as No Damage: 19,399 homes.
  • A view is a square centred on the inspector’s point, 20, 40 or 60 m wide on the ground, saved as a JPEG of 224 × 224 pixels. Cut the 20 m view from zoom 20 and the two others from zoom 19.
  • The flight missed some homes. Keep a home when the photo covers at least 99% of each of its three views: 8,183 Palisades homes and 11,083 Eaton homes. That leaves out 133 homes, each with a view the flight did not photograph at all.

The model

  • Everything runs in R on your laptop’s processor, with no account and no key: torch and torchvision for the model, magick for the views, sf and terra for the map. The first use of torch downloads about 500 MB.
  • The model is model_resnet18(pretrained = TRUE) with its last layer replaced by nn_identity(), in $eval() mode: 512 numbers for a view. Give it pixel values between 0 and 1, scaled by ImageNet’s means and standard deviations.
  • For each width, reduce the 512 numbers to 20 summaries with prcomp() at its default settings and fit a logistic regression of destroyed on them, both on the Palisades homes alone. An Eaton home is predicted destroyed above a probability of one half.
  • Nothing is drawn at random, and your number of homes should match mine exactly. The four counts in your tables can differ from mine by a few homes: a home with a probability close to one half changes side if its photo is cut or resized a little differently.

How long it took me

The first run, on a MacBook Pro with an M2 Max chip:

Step Minutes Downloaded
Download the inspections under 1 5 MB
Download the 34,032 photo tiles 5 1.13 GB
Cut the three views of each home 9
Run the 57,798 views through the model 27
Fit the regression and predict under 1
Download the map layers and draw the figure under 1 14 MB
Total about 42 1.15 GB

Save the result of each step. With everything saved, a second run takes under a minute.

Output

  • figures/hw6_target.png, 8.5 × 16.1 inches at 220 dpi.
  • data_clean/predictions.csv: one row per Eaton home kept, with CAL FIRE’s OBJECTID, the damage recorded, longitude and latitude, and at each width the probability and the cell.
  • NOTES.md: the number of homes at each step, the four counts at each width, your answer to the question below, and an AI disclosure (each tool with its model and version, what you used it for, what you checked).

What the model misses

Look at the photos of at least 20 false negatives (orange) and 20 false positives (yellow) at 20 m, starting with the ones the model was most sure of. In NOTES.md, name the reasons you see for each type of error and give the OBJECTID of two homes that show each reason. Then say in two or three sentences why the share correct falls as the photo widens.

Figure rules

  • A positive is a destroyed home. The figure has one title and a dark background, and the sources are at the bottom.
  • Panel A, a map of the Eaton fire. Each home is its parcel, coloured by its cell at 20 m: red for a true positive, orange for a false negative, yellow for a false positive, grey for a true negative, with a dot on every error. A home on a parcel larger than 5,000 m² is drawn as a dot. Behind the homes: the burned area, the relief coloured by altitude, roads and place names. The map has a key and a scale. A small map shows the coast with the sea in blue, both fires and the frame of the main map.
  • Panel B, a close-up of the area boxed in A, with home 6419 outlined.
  • Panel C, the Eaton home with OBJECTID 6419 at 20, 40 and 60 m. The 20 m square is marked on the two wider photos, and the share correct is written over each photo. Next to each photo, the four counts at that width in a 2 × 2 table: ground truth in rows, prediction in columns, the colours of the map.

Commits

Commit each step when it works. A healthy history for a project like this has a series of commits. For example:

  1. The project organized: folders, .gitignore, code/run_everything.R.
  2. The code that gets the data: inspections, tiles, views.
  3. The code that makes the prediction: the model’s numbers, the regression, the four cells.
  4. Panel A of the figure.
  5. Panel B.
  6. Panel C.
  7. A last pass that cleans up the code.

This order is just an example. You can adopt your own workflow.

How to submit

Your repository is hw6-<your github username> in the course organization. Put the code in code/, with code/run_everything.R running it all, and paths relative to the project root. Push the code, data_clean/predictions.csv, the figure, NOTES.md, and the files your figure script reads (the map layers and the three photos of home 6419, under 25 MB in all). The grader downloads nothing and runs no model, so the script that draws the figure must run from a fresh clone. The grader uses the last commit pushed before the deadline.
