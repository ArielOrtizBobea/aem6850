1 · What the voice carries

The 24 press conferences

The FOMC raised rates at 11 of its 24 meetings in 2021-2023. Jerome Powell, its chair, took reporters’ questions after every one.

A rate hike is a meeting that raised the target rate; a hold left it. A turn is one person’s unbroken stretch of talk; an answer is a Powell turn after his opening statement. Of 608 answers, 571 run 15 s or longer. We keep those, since shorter turns give too little sound to measure.

Dates: the Fed’s table of target-range changes. The labelled table of turns was built from the recordings before class and loads from file.

Two answers from one conference

A transcript gives the words of these two answers. Only the recording tells you the first sounds calm and the second activated, meaning animated and urgent.

What a voice carries beyond the words

A recording holds cues a transcript leaves out. Today puts numbers on some of them and leaves the rest aside.

  • Measured today
    • Pitch level: how high the voice is.
    • Pitch range: how much it moves, animated or flat.
    • Loudness, measured as energy, the size of the wave.
    • A model’s scores: arousal (how activated), valence (how positive).
  • Left aside today
    • Hesitation.
    • Voice quality: creaky or strained.
    • Accent, laughter and sarcasm.

Pitch range, first 15 s of the two answers just played: 16.1 Hz in the calm one and 34.0 Hz in the activated one. Hz: cycles a second.

Pitch and energy tracks of one answer

A recording holds tracks beyond the words. These 15 s have a pitch level of 109.9 Hz, a pitch range of 34.0 Hz and a mean energy of 55.7 dB.

A pitch tracker is software that estimates pitch in each 5-ms slice. A voiced frame is a slice where the vocal folds vibrate: vowels yes, “s” and “t” no. Pitch level is the median over voiced frames; pitch range is their interquartile range, the width of the middle half. Energy is the size of the wave in decibels (dB), heard as loudness; halving the wave takes off 6 dB. Powell, 3 Nov 2021, 25:15. Plotting code: code/1_pitch_energy.R.

A tracker can jump to double the true pitch. The median and the interquartile range both ignore a few jumps. 51% of these frames are voiced.

Arousal and valence

Emotion research scores speech on two scales. Arousal is how activated a speaker sounds, valence how positive. An emotion model returns both, roughly from 0 to 1. The red dots are the two answers you just heard.

First 15 s of each of Powell’s 571 answers, one dot each. The answer at 25:15 of 3 Nov 2021 has the highest arousal of the 571. A third score, dominance, correlates 0.94 with arousal across answers, so we show two. Correlation runs from −1 to 1: how closely two lists move together.

A speaker’s baseline and the z-score

Speakers differ far more than one speaker varies. So a measure is read against that speaker’s own usual level, the baseline.

A z-score is the distance from the baseline in standard deviations: (109.9 − 103.7) / 4.3 = +1.4. Press conference of 3 Nov 2021; right panel, the first 15 s of each of Powell’s answers of 15 s or more.

How a recording changes a voice measure

Powell measures 63.3 dB on 15 Jun 2022 and 47.7 dB on 20 Sep 2023, a factor of 6 in the wave’s height. His pitch level differs by only 2.3 Hz. That gap in loudness points to the recording.

How much of a voice measure is the speaker, and how much is the recording?

Gain: a multiplier applied to every sample, set at the sound desk, the mixer between microphone and video. Both clips are Powell at a press conference.

2 · The voice as research data

Vocal pitch in Congress

Small rises in a legislator’s pitch measure emotional intensity. Women in Congress show more of it when speaking about women.

  • Dietrich, Hayes & O’Brien (APSR, 2019). Women averaged 206.01 Hz when mentioning women and 203.27 Hz otherwise.
  • A recording archive with no labels can answer a question about behaviour once the comparison is built in.

Floor speech: a speech in the chamber during debate; here speeches of 50 or more words, Jan 2009 to Aug 2014. Praat is free software for analysing speech.

The authors argue that small pitch changes are hard for a speaker to control.

Tone of voice and share prices

A more positive tone in the Fed chair’s voice raises share prices over the following days. The Fed’s actions and the sentiment of its texts are held fixed.

  • Gorodnichenko, Pham & Talavera (AER, 2023). After five days, about 100 basis points per unit of tone, or about 75 per SD. No significant effect on the same day.
  • How a message is spoken can be a variable in an economics paper, so the quality of the score matters.

Text sentiment: how positive the written words are. Basis point: one hundredth of a percentage point.

Listeners rating voices

Anonymous listeners judged speakers’ race from the voice alone. Wages differ by how accurately listeners identified the speaker.

  • Grogger (JHR, 2011). Distinctly identified: four or more of five listeners judged the speaker’s race accurately.
  • Ratings by listeners can be the measure in a published study. You will rate clips by ear later today.

3 · Measuring pitch and loudness in R

Pitch of a known tone

Can we measure how a voice sounds without a model? Yes, in R, and the reading only means something against the speaker’s own baseline. First the tool: a fixed rule, with no training. A 120 Hz tone made in R comes back from the pitch tracker ksvF0() as 120 Hz.

R Console, in the 20-audio-ii folder. Installs wrassp and tuneR if missing; writes data_clean/tone120.wav
want <- c("wrassp", "tuneR")
need <- want[!(want %in% installed.packages()[,"Package"])]
if (length(need)) install.packages(need)
lapply(want, function(i) require(i, character.only = TRUE))
rm(want, need)
x <- round(8000 * sin(2 * pi * 120 * (1:48000) / 16000))     # 3 s at 120 Hz
writeWave(Wave(x, samp.rate = 16000, bit = 16), "data_clean/tone120.wav")
p <- ksvF0("data_clean/tone120.wav", gender = "m", toFile = FALSE)
length(p$F0)             # 600: one value every 5 ms
median(p$F0[p$F0 > 0])   # 120.03
  • wrassp measures pitch and energy in sound files. tuneR reads and writes them.
  • sin() builds 3 s at 16,000 samples a second.
  • gender = "m" sets the search range to 50-400 Hz, which fits Powell.

Pitch and energy of one clip

Two calls measure any WAV file. voice() reduces them to pitch level, pitch range, mean energy and voiced share.

R Console, in the 20-audio-ii folder; wrassp from the slide before. The clip: 3 Nov 2021, 25:15, 15 s
f <- "data/clips/activated.wav"
p <- ksvF0(f, gender = "m", toFile = FALSE)
round(p$F0[786:793], 1)     # 3.93 to 3.96 s: zeros, then voice starts
#> [1]   0.0   0.0 135.6 137.9 137.5 137.4 139.5 137.9
voice <- function(f, from = 0, to = 0) {
  p <- ksvF0(f, beginTime = from, endTime = to, gender = "m", toFile = FALSE)
  e <- rmsana(f, beginTime = from, endTime = to, toFile = FALSE)
  v <- p$F0[p$F0 > 0]       # the voiced frames
  c(pitch = median(v), range = IQR(v), energy = mean(e$rms), voiced = mean(p$F0 > 0))
}
round(voice(f), 2)
#>  pitch  range energy voiced 
#> 109.94  34.01  55.67   0.51

The clip is Powell’s activated answer from the press conference of 3 Nov 2021. Range is the interquartile range, IQR(). F0 is the pitch track, zero where no pitch was found. rmsana() returns energy in dB every 5 ms. A start and end of 0 mean the whole file, so voice() also serves one turn. It runs in about 0.01 s.

Halving the volume

Recordings differ in volume, so we test which measures depend on it. Halving every sample cuts energy by 6 dB. Pitch level moves under 1 Hz.

R Console, in the 20-audio-ii folder; f and voice() from the slide before; writes data_clean/half.wav
w <- readWave(f)
w@left <- as.integer(round(w@left / 2))    # every sample cut in half
writeWave(w, "data_clean/half.wav")
round(rbind(as_recorded = voice(f), half = voice("data_clean/half.wav")), 2)
#>              pitch range energy voiced
#> as_recorded 109.94 34.01  55.67   0.51
#> half        110.57 33.90  49.65   0.50
  • w@left holds the samples.
  • So energy depends on the recording’s volume, and pitch level does not.

4 · Emotion and tone models

What emotion and tone models are

Pitch and energy came from a fixed rule. Emotion and tone models are audio models trained to measure how speech sounds. Speech-to-text, also an audio model, writes the words instead.

Model Returns Compared with
Emotion model scores on scales: arousal 0.547, valence 0.507 what listeners hear
Tone classifier a label: positive, neutral or negative what listeners hear
Speech-to-text words: “That’s a decision for the Committee.” a transcript

A transcript records what was said. Nothing records how speech sounds, so these models can only be compared with listeners.

Valence is close to text sentiment, read from the sound. Arousal has no counterpart in text. Scores and words: Powell at 25:15, press conference of 3 Nov 2021.

How an emotion model is trained

Clips of speech are rated on scales, and a model learns to predict the ratings. It returns a new clip’s position on those scales.

  • Training data, the examples a model learns from, comes in two types:
    • Natural speech: recorded for another purpose, then rated by listeners. Today’s model learned from this.
    • Acted speech: actors perform an emotion on request, such as “happy” or “angry”. The request is the label.
  • The scale comes from those ratings, so it has no fixed meaning for a Fed chair.

Arousal: how activated a speaker sounds. Dominance: how in control. Valence: how positive. Powell, the Fed’s chair, 25:15 into a press conference. Today’s model: Wagner et al. (TPAMI, 2023), read as the arXiv preprint.

How a tone classifier is scored

The study on the next slide tests tone classifiers: each sentence gets a probability of positive, neutral and negative. AUC scores how well those probabilities rank the sentences.

Probability of “negative” Other sentence: 0.7 Other sentence: 0.6 Other sentence: 0.2
Truly negative sentence: 0.9 right right right
Truly negative sentence: 0.4 wrong wrong right
  • Pair each truly negative sentence with each other sentence. A pair is right when the truly negative one gets the higher probability.
  • AUC is the share of pairs right: 4 of 6 here, or 67. Repeat for each tone and average.
  • Guessing gets half the pairs right, so 50. A perfect model gets 100.
  • Accuracy would mislead: 89% of sentences on calls are neutral, so always answering “neutral” scores 89 with no skill. Its AUC is 50.

Made-up probabilities, to show the arithmetic. Listeners’ labels decide which sentences are truly negative. AUC: area under the curve.

Models trained on actors fail on executives

Many emotion and tone models learn from actors instead of rated natural speech. Tested on executives, they do little better than guessing. One retrained on calls does better.

  • What the models classify: is a sentence’s tone of voice positive, neutral or negative? The right answer is the emotion the actor was asked to perform, or for executives, the label human listeners gave.
  • Today’s model learned from podcast clips, so this is a caution by analogy. Test a score on speech like yours.

AUC as on the slide before: 50 is guessing, 100 is perfect. Ewertz, Knickrehm, Nienhaus & Reichmann (JAR, 2026), Table 2, p. 245, charted by us: 5,000 sentences from quarterly earnings calls.

Emotion scores for the 24 press conferences

Its authors trained today’s emotion model on podcast clips. We only ran it, before class, on the audio of all 24 press conferences, one 10-s window at a time. It runs in Python, so R only reads its scores. You test those scores by ear in the next part.

tools/emotion_windows.py (part, simplified): ran before class, about half a minute per conference; not run in class, and in the zip for later
model_name = "audeering/wav2vec2-large-robust-12-ft-emotion-msp-dim"
W = int(10 * sr)                           # 10 s = 160,000 samples
for s in range(0, len(x), W):              # one window at a time
    seg = x[s:s + W]
    y = processor(seg, sampling_rate=sr)["input_values"][0].reshape(1, -1)
    out = model(torch.from_numpy(y).to(device))[1][0].detach().cpu().numpy()

A window is a fixed stretch scored as one piece. The answer at 25:15 starts at 1514.68 s, so the first window holds about 4 s of the reporter’s question. Clips are scored whole, with one 15-s window each (replies/emotion_clips.csv, replies/emotion_openings.csv). Halving the volume of the clip at 25:15 leaves its arousal score at 0.547 to 0.546, since the model rescales the sound before scoring it.

Arousal follows pitch and energy, valence does not

The model returns two scores. Arousal is how activated a speaker sounds, and valence is how positive. Do they repeat what R can measure? Pitch level, pitch range and energy explain 37% of arousal and 7% of valence.

  • Each dot is one of Powell’s answers: our own calculation on today’s data.
  • So measures from R can partly check arousal. Valence needs something else: the model picks up word information carried in the sound (Wagner et al., TPAMI, 2023).

Each answer averages its 10-s windows. 571 answers, each measure z-scored within its conference. Explain: the R-squared of a regression. In Canada’s House of Commons, transcripts capture sentiment and miss arousal (Cochrane et al., Political Communication, 2022).

5 · The emotion score against your ear

Rating arousal by ear

Ratings by ear on a random sample tell us how far a score can be trusted on this speaker. You rate before seeing any score.

  • Rate the voice, setting the content aside as far as you can.
  • The model is already trained, on podcast clips, with no record on this speaker. Your ratings only check it.
  • The clips play from the instructor’s laptop. You may instead use earbuds and the numbered buttons on the session page.

Eight clips drawn at random

A seeded draw from Powell’s 571 answers picks the clips, so nobody chooses where the model looks good. You will rate them by ear, and cor() compares your ratings with the emotion model’s scores.

7 · Sound beyond speech

Sound other than speech can measure things reports miss. The source of a measure can set the result.

  • Carr & Doleac (REStat, 2018). Gunfire sensors (ShotSpotter) triangulate gunshots, and technicians verify them.
  • Müller et al. (Nature Communications, 2023). A soundscape is all the sound at a place over time. The sound model’s bird scores explain 69% of the variation in the animal communities experts identify, which track forest recovery after farming.

Curfew: a rule keeping minors indoors at night. 911: the US emergency number.
