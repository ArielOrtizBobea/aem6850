26 · Drone & satellite imagery

Tuesday, Dec 1, 2026

Slides · Live script · Source · the project

In this session we work with images from cameras on drones and satellites. A camera is a passive sensor. It records the sunlight that the ground reflects, in separate bands. We read a drone photo of a corn field in R and average it to satellite-sized cells. Then we read one Sentinel-2 image of Cayuga Lake, taken on 15 August 2024. We turn its stored numbers into reflectance and compute NDVI, a vegetation index. Published papers used images like these to measure growth, sprawl, crop output and crop burning. We compare sources by pixel size and by how often they pass, and we see where error enters a number built from images. Last, we measure the lake’s area three ways and train a decision tree on 200 points I labelled on aerial photos.

The project. Download the zip on this page, unzip it and open 26-satellite-local.Rproj. R then starts in the project folder, and every path on the slides is relative to it. The scripts install terra, sf and rpart if they are missing. I downloaded every file before class, so you do not need an account, a key or the network. The folder’s README.md says where each file came from and under what licence. The code that downloaded the files needs the network, so it is not in the zip. This page shows its main steps.

Objectives

After the 75 minutes you can:

  1. Explain the difference between passive and active sensors.
  2. Explain what a camera on a drone, a plane or a satellite records: bands of visible light, near-infrared light and heat, and lights at night.
  3. Choose imagery for a research question from its resolution in space (the pixel size), its resolution in time (how often an image is taken, and how often it is clear) and its bands.
  4. Read a drone image and a satellite image in R, and compute a vegetation index (NDVI) from two bands.
  5. Explain how measurement error enters a variable built from images (pixel size, clouds, classification mistakes), and measure it against an outside source.
  6. Describe how published papers used satellite images to measure economic growth, urban sprawl and crop burning.

This session leaves out radar and LiDAR, and jobs that process thousands of images at once.

Passive and active sensors

Sensors on satellites work in two ways.

  • A passive sensor records energy from the ground, such as reflected sunlight or heat. A camera is a passive sensor.
  • An active sensor sends its own signal and times what comes back. Radar and LiDAR are active sensors.
  • Radar works at night and through cloud.
  • Today we work with cameras.

Definitions from NASA Earthdata on remote sensing.

NASA Earthdata defines the two types this way: “Active instruments emit energy and collect data based on changes in the return signal”, and passive instruments “detect energy emitted or reflected from an object”. The NASA page names no instruments, so radar and LiDAR as active sensors are plain facts here.

Cameras on drones, planes and satellites

Cameras fly on drones, planes and satellites. A pixel is one cell of an image.

  • A drone photographed one corn field in North Carolina at 4.1 cm.
  • A plane photographs the lower 48 states at least every 3 years for USDA’s aerial photos (NAIP), at 0.6 m.
  • The Sentinel-2 satellites photograph almost all the world’s land about every 5 days, at 10 to 60 m.

Drone: NC State Extension via OpenAerialMap, CC BY 4.0. Photo: USGS, USDA, The National Map. Contains modified Copernicus Sentinel data (2024).

USDA is the US Department of Agriculture. NAIP is its National Agriculture Imagery Program.

Panel c is the same 200 m square in our Sentinel-2 image of 15 August 2024, in cells of 20 m. The three panels are printed at the same size, so only the ground in each pixel changes.

Cayuga Lake on a cloudy day

On 30 August 2024 the camera saw almost only cloud over Cayuga Lake. Two days later, radar showed the lake’s outline.

  • Sentinel-2 carries a camera. The European Space Agency (ESA) marks about 99% of the land and water around the lake as cloud that day.
  • Sentinel-1 carries a radar. Its radio waves pass through cloud.
  • Calm water reflects the radar signal away from the satellite, so the lakes look dark.

Contains modified Copernicus Sentinel data (2024). Terrain-corrected radar from Microsoft’s Planetary Computer.

ESA’s class for each cell puts 98.6% of the image in its two cloud classes (8 and 9) on 30 August, and 98.9% with thin, high cloud (class 10). On why calm water looks dark, see NASA Earthdata’s page on reading radar images.

I downloaded the camera image from the Earth Search catalog, which needs no account. The radar image comes from Microsoft’s Planetary Computer (collection sentinel-1-rtc, 1 September 2024), and I put it on our 20 m cells. That collection’s catalog record says it needs an account, yet the search and the download worked without one. The collection sentinel-1-grd has no such flag and has an image of 20 August 2024. Both downloads need the network, so they do not run here. The camera image came from this code:

What a band records

A camera records light in separate bands.

  • A band is one range of wavelengths. The image stores one layer per band.
  • Wavelength is measured in nanometres (nm), billionths of a metre. Our eyes see about 400 to 700 nm.
  • Sentinel-2 adds near-infrared and shortwave infrared, which our eyes cannot see.
  • Other sensors record heat the ground gives off, or lights from towns after dark.

B8A is Sentinel-2’s narrow near-infrared band. Band centres from ESA.

Our Sentinel-2 file keeps five of the satellite’s 13 bands: B02 blue (490 nm), B03 green (560 nm), B04 red (665 nm), B8A near-infrared (865 nm) and B11 shortwave infrared (1,610 nm). The drone’s bands are drawn as a typical camera’s red, green and blue. Its own response curves are not published.

Two other sensors appear later in the session. Landsat records heat in a thermal band with 100 m pixels. The VIIRS sensor records lights at night in one band from about 500 to 900 nm.

A drone photographed a corn trial in North Carolina in November 2024.

  • Each pixel covers 4.1 cm of ground. We can see single rows of corn.
  • Software stitches the overlapping drone photos into one image. This image is called an orthomosaic.
  • The width of ground in one pixel is called the ground sample distance.
  • The image covers 278 m by 287 m, or 7.96 hectares.

NC State Extension (the state university’s farm advisers) flew it on 12 November 2024 with a small consumer drone. Imagery via OpenAerialMap, CC BY 4.0.

The orthomosaic keeps true distances on the ground, like a map. A hectare is 100 m by 100 m.

The field is in Lenoir, Caldwell County, North Carolina (about 35.92 N, 81.52 W). Henderson, Regan and Venables traced Nairobi’s buildings from aerial photographs of 2003 and 2015, the same type of picture from a plane.

Reading the drone image in R

In R, a drone image is a raster with three layers: red, green and blue.

  • 12.2 of the 47.1 million pixels (26%) are black corners outside the flight.
  • The file does not mark these pixels as missing. They have 0 in every layer.
  • mask() sets every pixel with 0 in all three layers to NA. Averages then skip these pixels.

A Census road on the drone image

The Census line for the road next to the field is about 4 m off the road in the image.

  • TIGER is the Census Bureau’s map file of US roads. It has one line per road.
  • The line is 4.1 to 4.5 m from the centre of the road in the image.
  • The road is 6.8 to 6.9 m wide, so the line runs along its edge.
  • The drone’s GPS and the TIGER line can each be off by a few metres.

I measured at 20, 50 and 80% of the length of Pennell St NE. The flight’s record does not say whether it used ground control points (marked spots with surveyed coordinates).

Georeferencing pins an image to the map. Drone software places each photo with the drone’s own GPS, which can be off by a few metres. Ground control points tie the mosaic to true coordinates. These are spots marked on the ground and surveyed before the flight. A road line from another source shows how far off a mosaic is. A gap of 4 m can also come from the Census line. The Census Bureau draws its TIGER lines for maps, which need less accuracy than survey work.

The code below comes from code/2_drone.R. At three points along the TIGER line, it reads the pixels on a line of 50 m across the road, one every 4 cm. Asphalt here is blue-grey, so its blue layer is higher than its red layer. The road is the nearest stretch of asphalt wider than 3 m.

R Console, in the 26-satellite-local folder; mo from the slides before
rd <- st_read("data_clean/drone/caldwell_roads_near_mosaic.gpkg", quiet = TRUE)
ln <- st_cast(st_intersection(st_union(rd[rd$FULLNAME == "Pennell St NE", ]),
                              st_as_sfc(st_bbox(ext(mo) - 10, crs = st_crs(rd)))), "LINESTRING")[1]
off <- do.call(rbind, lapply(c(0.2, 0.5, 0.8), function(fr) {
  p0 <- st_coordinates(st_line_sample(ln, sample = fr))[1, 1:2]
  p1 <- st_coordinates(st_line_sample(ln, sample = fr + 0.01))[1, 1:2]
  tv <- (p1 - p0) / sqrt(sum((p1 - p0)^2)); nv <- c(-tv[2], tv[1])   # unit normal, left of the line
  s  <- seq(-25, 25, by = 0.04)
  v  <- extract(mo, cbind(p0[1] + s * nv[1], p0[2] + s * nv[2]))
  asph <- as.vector(stats::filter((v$blue > v$red) * 1, rep(1 / 25, 25)) > 0.5)
  asph[is.na(asph)] <- FALSE
  r <- rle(asph); en <- cumsum(r$lengths); stt <- en - r$lengths + 1
  runs <- data.frame(from = s[stt], to = s[en])[r$values, ]
  k <- 1
  while (k < nrow(runs)) {                       # join the two lanes across the centre stripe
    if (runs$from[k + 1] - runs$to[k] < 1) {
      runs$to[k] <- runs$to[k + 1]; runs <- runs[-(k + 1), ]
    } else k <- k + 1
  }
  runs <- runs[runs$to - runs$from > 3, ]
  runs <- runs[which.min(pmin(abs(runs$from), abs(runs$to),
                              ifelse(runs$from < 0 & runs$to > 0, 0, Inf))), ]
  data.frame(point = fr, width_m = round(runs$to - runs$from, 1),
             offset_to_centre_m = round((runs$from + runs$to) / 2, 1))
}))

The image of 15 August 2024

I searched Earth Search, a free catalog, for Sentinel-2 images of the lake from 10 to 20 August 2024. Only 15 August was clear.

  • Sentinel-2 has two satellites. Together they photograph each place about every 5 days.

The catalog I used, Earth Search, needs no account. The box runs from Ithaca to the lake’s north end: 1,490 columns and 2,916 rows of 20 m cells, or 29.8 km by 58.3 km. In longitude and latitude it runs from −76.815 to −76.438 and from 42.425 to 42.955.

Two orbits reach our tile. One photographs all of our image every 5 days. The other covers about three quarters of it in between. So in August 2024 a picture came every 2 or 3 days. ESA says the five-day revisit is “even faster at higher latitudes” (ESA).

The search needs the network and the rstac package. It asks the Earth Search catalog for every Sentinel-2 image over the box from 10 to 20 August 2024. The cloud share is the tile’s, in percent. Then the code reads only our box from each band file, over the web, without downloading the whole tile. The 10 m bands are averaged to 20 m.

Reading the image in R

Our image is a raster of 20 m cells with six layers. Five are bands, and the sixth is ESA’s class for each cell.

  • The file stores reflectance times 10,000 as whole numbers. Dividing by 10,000 gives reflectance.
  • Reflectance is the share of sunlight a surface sends back, from 0 to 1.
  • The bands are B02 blue, B03 green, B04 red, B8A near-infrared and B11 shortwave infrared.
  • SCL is ESA’s class for each cell. 4 is vegetation, 5 is not vegetated (bare ground, roofs and roads), and 6 is water.

Crop output from NDVI

Crop burning in Punjab

Jack and coauthors paid farmers in Punjab not to burn rice straw. A classifier measured burning on each field.

  • Question: Do payments to farmers reduce crop burning? Jack, Jayachandran, Kala & Pande (American Economic Review: Insights, 2025) asked this in India.

  • Data: 1,668 farmers in Punjab in 2019. Images at 3 m and 10 m.

A classifier puts each field into a class, here burned or unburned, from its band values.

Types of resolution

NASA lists four types of resolution. Today we use the first three.

  • Spatial resolution is the pixel size, 4.1 cm for the corn-field drone.
  • Temporal resolution is the time between images. Sentinel-2 passes over the lake about every 5 days.
  • Spectral resolution is the number and width of bands. The drone has 3 and Sentinel-2 has 13.
  • Radiometric resolution is the number of values a pixel can take. One series of lights at night stores only 0 to 63.

NASA Earthdata on remote sensing. NASA defines temporal resolution by the orbit. Here it is the time between images.

Measurement error in two reviews

Two reviews of satellite data in economics warn that numbers from images carry error.

  • Measurement error is the gap between our number and the true number.
  • Donaldson & Storeygard (Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2016) expect “false positives and false negatives” from any classification (p. 192).

Forest loss in Mexico

A satellite map misses about half of Mexico’s forest loss. Modelling that error almost triples the payments’ effect.

  • Question: Mexico pays landowners to keep forest. Did the payments cut forest loss? Alix-García & Millimet (Journal of the Association of Environmental and Resource Economists, 2023).
  • Method: They modelled missed loss and false loss.
  • Data: 20,919 parcels, 2001 to 2014, and a 30 m Landsat map.
  • Result: The usual estimate cuts the yearly chance of loss by 0.5 points, not significant. With the errors modelled, the cut is 1.4 points.

Each dot is a 5 km cell. Across is its forest share on a Landsat map (2010), and up is its share on the government’s map (2011). Dots far from the diagonal are cells where the two maps disagree.

Classical error. Classical measurement error is noise unrelated to the true value. A yes/no map’s error is tied to the truth. Where the truth is loss, the error can only lower the number. Where the truth is no loss, it can only raise it. So the usual fixes for classical error do not apply, and Alix-García & Millimet model the two error rates directly.
