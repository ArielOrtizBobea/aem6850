1 · Choosing how to read a document

Five routes from a page to a table

How the document was made decides which routes apply.

  • Born-digital PDF, made by software: the words are stored as characters
    • 1 · The text layer: those characters, copied out exactly
  • Scan of a printed page: a picture, pixels with no characters
    • 2 · OCR, optical character recognition: software that turns printed letters into characters
    • 3 · A vision model: an AI model that reads a page image into a table
  • Handwritten page: OCR reads it poorly
    • 3 · A vision model, as for a scan
    • 4 · Typing by hand, double keyed: two people type each page, differences re-read
  • Any of the three
    • 5 · Already typed by someone else: an archive, volunteers, a paper’s posted data

Cost per page: the text layer nothing, OCR seconds, a vision model cents, typing by hand dollars. Hand entry is still the standard for small high-stakes tables; double keying is how data-entry firms price it.

Bank balance sheets read by OCR

  • The question in Correia, Luck & Verner (QJE, 2026): why do banks fail, and can a failure be seen coming in a bank’s own published accounts?
  • The route: OCR on the balance sheet of every national bank, printed each year in the reports of the Comptroller of the Currency, the US office that supervises those banks, up to 1941, joined to the banks’ later regulatory filings: a panel of US banks from 1863 to 2024. One of those pages is read three ways in Part 3.
  • The check: every balance sheet obeys assets = liabilities + capital, the accounting identity. Wherever OCR’s numbers broke it, a person re-read the page.
  • The finding: failing banks show rising losses and weakening solvency years ahead, so failures are predictable from public accounts.

Left: printed page 646 of the 1899 report, from FRASER, the digital archive of the Federal Reserve Bank of St. Louis; a US government publication, so public domain.

The identity is the paper’s quality control: it tells you which OCR rows to re-read by hand without reading all of them.

India’s statistical volumes typed by hand

  • The question in Donaldson (AER, 2018): how large were the gains from a transport network, and where did they come from? The case: the railroads built across British-ruled India from 1853 on.
  • The route: the government’s printed statistical volumes, which nobody had digitized, entered page by page into a panel of 235 districts by year: crop prices, output, daily rainfall and trade.
  • The finding: a railroad’s arrival cut trade costs and price gaps between regions, raised trade, and raised real incomes in the districts it reached.

Left: Figure 1 of the paper, the railroad network (thick lines) inside the outline of colonial India in 1860, 1880, 1900 and 1930.

AER: American Economic Review. The rainfall series matters later: the handwritten sheets in Part 4 are the raw form such series come from.

London’s weekly death reports typed by hand

  • The question in Hanlon (REStat, 2024): how many deaths did London’s coal-smoke fogs cause, and did the toll change as the city changed?
  • The route: weekly death counts typed from the printed weekly reports of the Registrar General, the office that recorded births and deaths in England; the daily weather notes in the same reports, read as text, date every heavy fog (right).
  • The finding: a fog week raised deaths, so trapped coal smoke killed; the toll was larger while measles and tuberculosis were common, and fell with them.

Right: heavy-fog weeks and days per year, Figure 11 of the paper’s working-paper version (NBER, 2018).

REStat: Review of Economics and Statistics. Two kinds of extraction on one source: numbers from the tables, dates from the text. The typed death counts are public at the UK Data Service.

2 · Reading a born-digital PDF

Born-digital PDFs and their challenges

A born-digital PDF stores every character, so R can read its text. Three things still stand between that text and a table, and the H.4.1 has all three:

  • The text comes as lines, not rows and columns: the table is rebuilt from the lines.
  • Numbers carry thousands commas and footnote marks such as “(0)”, and a minus sign can sit apart from its number.
  • A long table runs across pages, its header repeated on each.

Total assets in the Fed’s balance sheet

The first number to pull out of the PDF: Total assets, on page 6 of the H.4.1.

Table 5, the balance sheet of the twelve Reserve Banks combined, in millions of dollars, as of 2 October 2024 (the release came out on the 3rd); page 7 prints Total liabilities and Total capital.

7,046,925 million is about 7.0 trillion dollars.

Finding a row in the text layer

A printed row is one line of the text, so finding a row is a search for its label.

R Console, in the 18-documents folder; needs pdftools
library(pdftools)                              # reads the text stored in a PDF
txt <- pdf_text("data/h41.pdf")                # the H.4.1, the Fed's balance sheet: one string per page
length(txt)
#> [1] 11
lines <- strsplit(txt[6], "\n")[[1]]           # page 6 (Table 5), one element per printed line
length(lines)
#> [1] 39
line <- grep("Total assets", lines, value = TRUE)   # the line that holds the Total assets row
line
#> [1] "Total assets                                                           (0)            7,046,925         -    33,134           -   908,857"

pdf_text() keeps the layout: the columns arrive as runs of spaces; on this line the amount in millions of dollars, then the changes on the week and on the year.

The spaces are what make the columns; they are not reliable enough to split on blindly, which is why the next slide uses a pattern.

Pulling the amount out with a capture group

R Console, in the 18-documents folder; needs stringr
library(stringr)
m <- str_match(line, "Total assets\\s+\\(0\\)\\s+([0-9,]+)")   # ( ) captures the amount on the Total assets line
m
#>      [,1]                                                                                             
#> [1,] "Total assets                                                           (0)            7,046,925"
#>      [,2]       
#> [1,] "7,046,925"
assets <- as.numeric(gsub(",", "", m[, 2]))    # drop the thousands commas
assets
#> [1] 7046925

A capture group, the part of the pattern in parentheses, turns one line into one number; \\s+ is any run of spaces. The “(0)” after the label is a footnote marker the Fed prints; the pattern steps over it. The amount is in millions, so 7,046,925 is about $7.0 trillion.

str_match returns a matrix: the whole match, then one column per group.

amount <- function(label, lines) {
  l <- grep(label, lines, value = TRUE)[1]
  as.numeric(gsub(",", "", str_match(l, paste0(label, "\\s+(?:\\([0-9]+\\)\\s+)?([0-9,]+)"))[, 2]))
}
liabilities <- amount("Total liabilities", lines7)

#> liabilities     capital      assets 
#>     7003485       43440     7046925

3 · Reading a scan of a printed page

Scans of printed pages and their challenges

A scan is a picture of a printed page: the file holds pixels, so there is no text to copy until OCR turns the letter shapes into characters. Archives often run OCR before posting a scan, as FRASER did for the Comptroller’s reports.

  • The errors are silent: a misread digit looks like any other number.
  • OCR misreads digits, merges columns and drops rows, more often on a faint, skewed or low-resolution scan.
  • An archive’s OCR text looks like a text layer, misreads included.

How OCR and a vision model read a page

OCR matches the letter shapes of each word and scores its confidence; a vision model reads squares of the image with your instructions and writes the reply in the format you ask for.

OCR Vision model
Reads print, word by word print, layout, handwriting
A misread garbled, scored low: bovL4 at 0 a plausible number, unscored
Two runs the same text can differ
Runs on your computer, free a provider’s servers, billed per token

The row is the last one of the 1899 bank table that follows, scanned at 300 dpi. Top: tesseract’s own word boxes and confidences (ocr_data()); it wrote 4,708.0” for 4,708.6 and bovL4 for 1,071.4, both scored low, and 87.8, right, also at 21. Bottom: the model’s saved reading of the same row, every number right. The reply’s words are tokens too, and each is billed.

The squares are 28 pixels a side, as the provider documents; the full 1899 page holds about 5,400 of them, so it is shrunk slightly to the 4,784 a request takes at most.

4 · Reading a handwritten page

Handwritten pages and their challenges

A handwritten page is kept as an image, a scan or a photograph, like a printed one. The writing is what makes it harder:

  • OCR, built for print, reads handwriting as noise.
  • Each writer forms digits a little differently, so 2.55 and 2.65 can look alike.
  • The layout varies from sheet to sheet: a brace across two months, a blank cell, a note beside a number.
  • A vision model can drop a cell or write a number that is not on the page, and the written totals do not catch every such number.

Rainfall sheets typed by volunteers

The 1870s Abbotsbury sheet; Met Office archive, via Rainfall Rescue (CC BY 4.0).

  • A rainfall sheet is a 12-by-10 table written by an observer: months as rows, years as columns, inches of rain (1 inch = 25.4 mm), and a Total row for each year.
  • The Met Office, the UK’s weather service, holds about 66,000 such sheets, 1677 to 1960.
  • In 2020, 16,000 volunteers typed them in a few weeks on Zooniverse, a citizen-science site, several people per sheet, entries reconciled: open data, Hawkins et al. (Geoscience Data Journal, 2023).
  • The 18-documents folder holds five sheets from Abbotsbury Castle, on the Dorset coast of southern England, the 1860s to the 1910s, with the volunteers’ file for each; the handwriting changes between decades.

Rainfall Rescue ran from March to April 2020. Ed Hawkins (University of Reading) led it.

Which check catches each model error

Summing the months catches a doubled figure. It misses a total the model invented for a year the sheet leaves blank: with no months to add, the check returns NA, as it does for any blank year.

The model’s errors, one call on each of the five sheets Cells Caught by
Left empty by the model where the volunteers typed a number 0 the month sum falls short of the written total
A figure the observer wrote across two months (a brace), copied by the model into both 4 the month sum overshoots the written total
-1.00 written by the model as a year’s total where the sheet says N.R., no record 3 only a second reading, or the image
Different numbers in the two readings 7 the second reading; the month sum when the error is large

A dropped cell is a number the model left out; the sum falls short. An invented number is a value that is not on the page, such as the -1.00 the model wrote for 1912, 1913 and 1917, where the 1910s sheet says N.R., no record. Only a second reading, or the image, catches it.

A second run wrote 0.00 into all thirteen cells of an empty year: replies differ between runs, which is why every reply is saved.

Cost per page by reader and model size

A model bills by the tokens it reads and writes, pieces of image and text: a rainfall sheet costs a tenth of a cent with the small model and 3 cents with the large.

# $ per million tokens read, written (provider's list, 29 Oct 2026); / 1e4 below gives cents
PRICE <- rbind(small = c(0.10, 0.50), medium = c(2, 10), large = c(4, 20))
log$cents <- (log$input_tokens * PRICE[log$size, 1] + log$output_tokens * PRICE[log$size, 2]) / 1e4
log$page <- ifelse(log$page == "1899", "1899 page", "rainfall sheet")
round(tapply(log$cents, list(log$size, log$page), mean), 2)   # cents per page, mean of the calls

A model takes about 10 seconds a sheet at any size; the hours are one call at a time, and eight calls at once cut them by eight. The 70,000 pages assume every volume is the size of 1899’s.

Choosing between an agent and a loop

A few pages go to an agent; a job you must rerun, audit or scale goes into a loop. Agent or loop is who runs the route: a coding agent told in a chat session what to do, or a script that loops over the pages, calls the model and saves every reply.

An agent in a session A loop in a script
Pages a few, up to some dozens hundreds to millions
Repeating it you, typing the request again; the answer may change anyone with the script, from the saved replies, identical
What is kept the files it wrote, if you ask every raw reply, the instructions, the model’s id
Cost per page the agent’s own calls, many per page, not itemised one model call, cents
Checks the ones you ask for, read in its reply the ones in the code, run on every page

Either way the test is a sum the page prints, recomputed from the numbers extracted.
