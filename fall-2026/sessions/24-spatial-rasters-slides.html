Cells, resolution and extent

A raster is a grid of equal cells. Each cell holds one number.

  • A cell (or pixel) is one square of the grid.
  • The resolution is the size of a cell: 1/24 of a degree, about 3.4 km by 4.6 km at Ithaca.
  • The extent is the rectangle the grid covers: here the lower 48 states.

PRISM’s daily maximum temperature, from Oregon State University. Warren Hall is a Cornell building in Ithaca, in Tompkins County, New York.

Reading one day of temperature

rast() reads the file. Its printout gives the grid’s size, resolution, extent, coordinate system and range of values.

  • A CRS (coordinate reference system) ties the numbers to places on Earth. NAD83 gives longitude and latitude in degrees.

One layer per day

One file can hold many layers on the same cells. tmax_july2012.tif holds 31, one for each day of July 2012.

  • A layer is one grid. A stack is several layers on the same cells.
  • Each layer is named by its date. PRISM’s day ends at 8 am in New York. The layer dated 18 July holds the afternoon of 17 July.
  • values() of the stack is a matrix of cells by days.
R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; i from the slide before; reads data_clean/tmax_july2012.tif
tmax <- rast("data_clean/tmax_july2012.tif")    # the 31 daily grids of July 2012 in one file
nlyr(tmax)
#> [1] 31
names(tmax)[c(1, 2, 31)]
#> [1] "2012-07-01" "2012-07-02" "2012-07-31"
range(time(tmax))                               # the dates, stored as dates
#> [1] "2012-07-01" "2012-07-31"
dim(values(tmax))                               # cells by days
#> [1] 872505     31
values(tmax)[i, 16:18]                          # Warren Hall's cell on 16, 17 and 18 July
#> 2012-07-16 2012-07-17 2012-07-18 
#>      28.59      30.98      35.82

The file stores hundredths of a degree, so 30.977 reads 30.98. I built it from PRISM’s 31 daily files with tools/make_clean.R.

Cropping and masking to New York

crop() keeps the rectangle of cells around New York. mask() then sets the cells outside the state to NA.

  • The Census Bureau’s county file has 3,235 counties, 62 of them in New York. The counties and the grid share one CRS, NAD83.
  • The crop has 109 rows by 190 columns, and it renumbers the cells. Warren Hall’s cell is now 11,669.
R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; tmax from the slide before; reads data/census/cb_2024_us_county_500k.zip
unzip("data/census/cb_2024_us_county_500k.zip", exdir = "data_clean/counties")
co  <- st_read("data_clean/counties/cb_2024_us_county_500k.shp", quiet = TRUE)   # county outlines

st_crs(ny) == st_crs(tmax)                      # do the counties and the grid share one CRS?

#> [1] 109 190  31

#> [1] 11669
tny_in <- mask(tny, ny)                         # NA in every cell whose centre is outside New York

The Census Bureau is the US statistics agency. A county is the level of government below the state. The bounding box is the smallest rectangle around the state.

Daily temperature at Warren Hall

extract() reads a point’s cell in every layer. Warren Hall’s hottest layer is the one dated 18 July, at 35.8 °C.

  • project() puts the point, built from longitude and latitude, in the grid’s CRS.
  • 13 of the 31 layers pass 30 °C. The month’s mean is 29.9 °C.
  • The same three lines serve any list of points, one row per point.
R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; tny from the slide before
wh <- vect(cbind(lon = -76.4797, lat = 42.4490), crs = "EPSG:4326")   # Warren Hall: longitude first
wh <- project(wh, crs(tny))                     # the point in the grid's coordinate system
wh_t <- unlist(extract(tny, wh, ID = FALSE))    # the value of Warren Hall's cell in each layer
round(wh_t[16:19], 1)
#> 2012-07-16 2012-07-17 2012-07-18 2012-07-19 
#>       28.6       31.0       35.8       29.7
data.frame(hottest = names(which.max(wh_t)), degrees = round(max(wh_t), 1),
           days_above_30 = sum(wh_t > 30))
#>      hottest degrees days_above_30
#> 1 2012-07-18    35.8            13

EPSG:4326 is the CRS of GPS coordinates. Web maps show latitude first, and vect() takes longitude first.

Overview of part 2

How to compute a new grid from the grids we have.

  • Arithmetic cell by cell: the 17 July layer against July’s mean.
  • Counting days: the days above 30 °C in each cell.
  • Wind speed and direction: from the two wind layers, at Warren Hall.

Later in this part: days above 30 °C across New York in July 2012, from 0 to 26.

17 July minus July’s mean

Arithmetic on rasters works cell by cell. In the 17 July layer, New York’s cells ran from 1.2 °C below their July mean to 4.6 °C above.

R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; tny, ny and wh from part 1
t17 <- tny[["2012-07-17"]]
dev <- t17 - mean(tny)                          # mean() of a stack averages its 31 layers
round(range(values(dev), na.rm = TRUE), 1)
#> [1] -1.2  4.6
g <- c(t17, mean(tny), dev); names(g) <- c("day", "month_mean", "difference")   # c() stacks layers
round(unlist(extract(g, wh, ID = FALSE)), 1)    # Warren Hall
#>        day month_mean difference 
#>       31.0       29.9        1.0
plot(g, nc = 3, fun = function() lines(vect(ny)))

tny is July’s 31 days cropped to New York and wh is Warren Hall’s point.

If useful: the difference map shows round spots around weather stations. PRISM estimates each cell from stations.

Counting days in each cell

  • Question: Schlenker & Roberts (PNAS, 2009) asked how temperature affects US corn, soybean and cotton yields.
  • Method: They built daily temperatures on a 4 km grid. In each cell they counted the time spent in each 1 °C interval, then averaged each county’s cropland cells.
  • Data: county yields, 1950 to 2005, east of the 100th meridian for corn and soybeans.
  • Result: Corn yields rise with temperature up to 29 °C and fall steeply above it. The threshold is 30 °C for soybeans and 32 °C for cotton.

A yield is the harvest per unit of land. The 100th meridian runs through the Great Plains.

Days above 30 °C in New York

tny > 30 is TRUE or FALSE in every cell and day, and sum() counts the TRUE days. New York’s cells had 0 to 26 days above 30 °C.

  • TRUE counts as 1 and FALSE as 0. sum() of a stack adds its layers in each cell and returns one layer.
  • Warren Hall’s cell gives 13, the count from its 31 values.
  • The most days fall along the Hudson valley north of Albany and in New York City. The fewest are in the Adirondacks.
R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; tny, ny and wh from part 1
hot <- sum(tny > 30)                            # TRUE or FALSE per cell and day; sum() counts TRUE
range(values(hot), na.rm = TRUE)
#> [1]  0 26
unlist(extract(hot, wh, ID = FALSE))            # Warren Hall
#> sum 
#>  13
plot(mask(hot, ny)); lines(vect(ny))

tny is July’s 31 days cropped to New York. 30 °C is a round number for this example.

Wind speed and direction

  • Question: Deryugina, Heutel, Miller, Molitor & Reif (American Economic Review, 2019) asked how fine particles affect deaths among the elderly.
  • Method: They computed the wind direction at each pollution monitor from the eastward and northward wind. Then they averaged each county’s monitors by day. The direction brings pollution from elsewhere, so it is their instrument.
  • Data: wind on a 32 km grid, and Medicare records by county and day, 1999 to 2013.
  • Result: One more microgram per cubic metre for one day caused 0.69 more deaths per million people over 65 within three days.

Medicare is US public health insurance for people 65 and over. Fine particles (PM2.5) are smaller than 2.5 micrometres. The figure is Figure 2, from PubMed Central. Its title is “Relationship between Daily Average Wind Direction and PM2.5 Concentrations for Counties in and around the Bay Area, CA”.

The wind at Warren Hall

Wind comes as two layers: u toward the east and v toward the north. Speed is sqrt(u^2 + v^2), and atan2() gives the direction.

  • At Warren Hall at 18 UTC on 17 July 2012, u was 4.8 m/s and v was 1.2 m/s. That is 17.7 km/h from 256°, a little south of west.
  • A wind is named by where it comes from. Directions count clockwise from north: 0 north, 90 east, 180 south, 270 west.
R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; wh from part 1; reads the two wind files in data_clean/
u    <- rast("data_clean/u10_ny_20120717.tif")  # wind toward the east, metres per second, one layer per hour
v10  <- rast("data_clean/v10_ny_20120717.tif")  # wind toward the north, metres per second
kmh  <- sqrt(u^2 + v10^2) * 3.6                 # speed: 1 metre per second is 3.6 km/h
from <- (atan2(-u, -v10) * 180 / pi) %% 360     # where the wind comes from, clockwise from north
now  <- c(u[["18:00"]], v10[["18:00"]], kmh[["18:00"]], from[["18:00"]])
names(now) <- c("u", "v", "kmh", "from")
round(unlist(extract(now, project(wh, crs(u)), ID = FALSE)), 1)    # Warren Hall at 18:00 UTC
#>     u     v   kmh  from 
#>   4.8   1.2  17.7 256.0

ERA5 is a weather model run over past observations, on 0.25-degree cells. 18 UTC is 2 pm at Ithaca in July.

Average u and v first, then compute the direction. Two winds from 350° and 10° average to north, and their two angles average to 180°. Practice task 6 shows it on the 24 hours.

3 · Combining rasters and polygons

Overview of part 3

How to average a grid’s cells over each county. A summary of the cells inside each county is a zonal statistic.

  • The problem: yields, deaths and incomes come by county. Weather comes by cell.
  • The packaged function: one mean per county, and the time it takes.
  • Inside the function: the cells of Tompkins County, and Exercise 2.
  • Every county and every day: all 3,109 counties of the lower 48 states.
  • Other weights: people in place of area.

Later in this part: the 107 cells that Tompkins County touches.

County averages of gridded weather

  • Question: Deschênes & Greenstone (American Economic Review, 2007) asked how climate change would affect US farm profits.
  • Method: For rainfall they laid a land-use map over PRISM’s 4 km cells and averaged the farmland cells of each county. Temperature came from weather stations. Then they compared each county’s profits in its hotter and cooler years.
  • Data: the Census of Agriculture of 1987 to 2002, PRISM for rainfall, and about 8,000 stations for temperature.
  • Result: Predicted warming would raise yearly farm profits by about $1.3 billion, or 4%.

A zonal statistic is a summary of the cells inside each zone, here the mean over a county. The map is Figure 2B of the working paper (NBER 10663), “Average Growing Season Total Precipitation, by County 1970-2000”. Fisher, Hanemann, Roberts & Schlenker (American Economic Review, 2012) later found errors in the paper’s county weather data.

County means with extract()

extract() with county outlines and fun = mean returns one mean per county. In the 17 July layer Tompkins County’s was 29.28 °C.

  • exact = TRUE counts a cell cut by the county line by the fraction of it inside the county.
  • na.rm = TRUE leaves out cells with no value. Without it, Erie and Jefferson counties get NA from their cells over the Great Lakes.
  • The 62 means run from 28.2 °C (Hamilton, in the Adirondacks) to 32.8 °C (Richmond, which is Staten Island).
R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; t17 and ny from the slides before
ny$t17 <- extract(t17, ny, fun = mean, exact = TRUE, na.rm = TRUE, ID = FALSE)[, 1]
round(ny$t17[ny$GEOID == "36109"], 2)           # Tompkins County
#> [1] 29.28
round(range(ny$t17), 1)
#> [1] 28.2 32.8
plot(ny["t17"], main = "")

t17 is the 17 July layer over New York and ny is its 62 counties. GEOID is the county code. 36109 is New York (36) and Tompkins (109).

Timing extract() on every US county

One call takes about 30 seconds, for 1 day of temperature or for 31. Three calls take three times as long.

  • I timed extract() with exact = TRUE, as on the slide before, on the 3,109 counties of the lower 48 states.
  • The time follows the number of calls. The number of days hardly matters.
  • At this rate, one call a month for 30 years takes about 3 hours.

A call is one run of extract(). I measured each case once on my laptop with tools/time_methods.R. The figure reads data_clean/timings.csv.

His question: “31 days in one call cost the same as one day. Three calls cost three times as much. So what does the function spend its time on?” Let them answer. The answer: the geometry of each call, which is finding the cells of each county and the fraction of each inside. Extra layers cost almost nothing. One call a day for a year would take about 3 hours (365 times 29 s is 2.9 hours, not measured).

The cells of Tompkins County

Tompkins County touches 107 cells. 58 lie wholly inside it, and the county line cuts the other 49.

  • The coverage fraction is the share of a cell’s area inside the county. A whole cell has 1.
  • Three cells are labelled with their number and fraction.
  • The fractions times the cells’ areas sum to 1,273.0 km². The Census Bureau gives 1,273.1 km² of land and water.

The table of cells and fractions

exact_extract() with include_cell = TRUE returns the table the mean is computed from: one row per cell that Tompkins County touches.

  • value is the cell’s temperature in the 17 July layer.
  • cell is the cell’s number in New York’s grid t17.
  • coverage_fraction is the share of the cell inside the county.
R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; t17 and ny from the slides before; exactextractr loaded
tk  <- ny[ny$GEOID == "36109", ]                            # Tompkins County
tab <- exact_extract(t17, tk, include_cell = TRUE)[[1]]     # one row per cell the county touches
head(tab, 3)
#>   value  cell coverage_fraction
#> 1 31.06 10905         0.1792120
#> 2 30.03 10906         0.4784630
#> 3 29.12 10907         0.4552244
c(cells = nrow(tab), whole = sum(tab$coverage_fraction == 1),
  partial = sum(tab$coverage_fraction < 1))
#>   cells   whole partial 
#>     107      58      49
f <- rast(t17)                                              # an empty grid with the same cells
f[tab$cell] <- tab$coverage_fraction                        # the fractions go in by cell number
plot(crop(f, tk, snap = "out")); lines(vect(tk))

The result is a list with one table per county, and [[1]] is Tompkins. The last three lines draw the map on the slide before.

Tompkins County’s mean by hand

The county mean is a weighted mean. Multiply each cell’s temperature by its fraction, add the products, and divide by the sum of the fractions.

  • A weighted mean is an average in which some values count more than others.
  • By hand: 29.279 °C, the number extract() returned.
R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; tab and ny from the slides before
x <- tab$value; w <- tab$coverage_fraction
sum(w * x) / sum(w)                             # value times fraction, over the sum of fractions
#> [1] 29.27899
ny$t17[ny$GEOID == "36109"]                     # extract()'s answer for Tompkins County
#> [1] 29.27899
mean(x)                                         # every cell counted as whole
#> [1] 29.23794

Analogy if useful: a course grade is a weighted mean, since the final counts more than one homework.

The 31 daily means as a matrix product

The 31 weighted means are one matrix product: a row of 107 weights times a matrix of 107 cells by 31 days.

  • The weights are the fractions over their sum. %*% multiplies the row by each column and adds the products.
R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; tny, tab and w from the slides before
Mny <- values(tny)                              # 20,710 cells by 31 days
w1  <- matrix(w / sum(w), nrow = 1)             # the weights as one row that sums to 1
dim(w1); dim(Mny[tab$cell, ])
#> [1]   1 107
#> [1] 107  31
out <- w1 %*% Mny[tab$cell, ]                   # (1 by 107) times (107 by 31) gives 1 by 31
round(out[1, 16:19], 2)
#> 2012-07-16 2012-07-17 2012-07-18 2012-07-19 
#>      26.85      29.28      34.12      28.16

The weight matrix P

For every county at once, the weights go in one matrix P, with a row per county and a column per cell.

  • For the lower 48 states P has 3,109 rows and 872,505 columns.
  • Row 2,063 is Tompkins County, with 107 weights and 872,398 zeros.
  • The figure draws New York’s P: 62 rows, 20,710 columns, a dot for each weight.

His question: “P has one row per county and one column per cell, and nearly every entry is zero. How should R store it?” Two slides on, the answer.

The table of cells for every county

The table of cells and fractions for all 3,109 counties takes about 3 seconds. Its weights are the entries of P.

R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; co and tmax from part 1; exactextractr loaded
low  <- co[!(co$STUSPS %in% c("AK", "HI", "PR", "GU", "VI", "AS", "MP")), ]    # lower 48 and DC
info <- exact_extract(tmax[[1]], low, include_cell = TRUE, progress = FALSE)   # a table per county
tabN <- data.frame(county = rep(1:nrow(low), sapply(info, nrow)),
                   do.call(rbind, info))        # stack the 3,109 tables into one
tabN <- tabN[!is.na(tabN$value), ]              # cells over water hold no temperature
tabN$w <- tabN$coverage_fraction / ave(tabN$coverage_fraction, tabN$county, FUN = sum)
c(counties = length(unique(tabN$county)), rows = nrow(tabN))
#> counties     rows 
#>     3109   567158
range(tapply(tabN$w, tabN$county, sum))         # each county's weights sum to 1
#> [1] 1 1

rep() writes each county’s number once per cell. ave(x, g, FUN = sum) puts each county’s sum on every row of that county.

Sparse matrices

A sparse matrix stores only the entries that are not zero: each one’s row, column and value.

  • The example has two counties, Tompkins and Cortland, and four cells of New York’s grid.
  • Cell 11,675 lies 35% in Tompkins and 65% in Cortland.
  • Five of the eight entries are not zero, so the table on the right has five rows.

P as a sparse matrix

P holds every county’s cell weights. It takes 10.3 MB as a sparse matrix, against 21.7 GB as an ordinary matrix.

  • i is the county’s row, j the cell’s number and x the weight.
  • The first call builds the small example. Dots are zeros.
R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; tabN, low and tmax from the slide before
sparseMatrix(i = c(1, 1, 1, 2, 2), j = c(1, 2, 3, 3, 4), x = c(1, 1, 0.35, 0.65, 1), dims = c(2, 4))
#> 2 x 4 sparse Matrix of class "dgCMatrix"
#>                
#> [1,] 1 1 0.35 .
#> [2,] . . 0.65 1
P <- sparseMatrix(i = tabN$county, j = tabN$cell, x = tabN$w, dims = c(nrow(low), ncell(tmax)))
c(rows = nrow(P), columns = ncol(P), not_zero = nnzero(P))
#>     rows  columns not_zero 
#>     3109   872505   567158
round(c(sparse_MB = as.numeric(object.size(P)) / 1e6, ordinary_GB = prod(dim(P)) * 8 / 1e9), 1)
#>   sparse_MB ordinary_GB 
#>        10.3        21.7

tabN is the table of every county’s cells and weights, and low is the 3,109 counties. A number takes 8 bytes: 3,109 × 872,505 × 8 bytes is 21.7 GB.

Every county’s mean on every day

P %*% M returns every county’s mean on every day, 3,109 counties by 31 days. It takes a few hundredths of a second once M is read.

  • M is July’s 31 layers as a matrix, 872,505 cells by 31 days (about 220 MB).
  • Tompkins County’s row matches the means computed by hand.
  • With P built, a month costs one values() (1.3 seconds) and the product.
R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; P, low and tmax from the slides before
M <- values(tmax)                               # 872,505 cells by 31 days
system.time(cd <- P %*% M)                      # counties by days
#>    user  system elapsed 
#>   0.022   0.000   0.023
round(cd[low$GEOID == "36109", 16:19], 2)       # Tompkins County
#> 2012-07-16 2012-07-17 2012-07-18 2012-07-19 
#>      26.85      29.28      34.12      28.16
ee <- exact_extract(tmax, low, "mean", progress = FALSE)    # the packaged function, all 31 days
max(abs(as.matrix(cd) - as.matrix(ee)))         # the largest difference between the two, degrees C
#> [1] 5.58982e-06

P holds the table of cells once. A new weight, such as people or cropland, changes one line. For the month, exact_extract() took 1.2 seconds and terra’s extract() 30. Clock: if Exercise 2 ended after minute 52, skip the next two slides (area and population weights).

Area weights or population weights

  • Question: Does a hotter year slow a country’s economic growth? Dell, Jones & Olken (American Economic Journal: Macroeconomics, 2012) asked it of 125 countries.
  • Method: They averaged the temperature cells of each country and year, weighting each cell by its 1990 population. Area weights serve as a second version.
  • Data: monthly temperature on a 0.5-degree grid (University of Delaware), 1950 to 2003.
  • Result: In poor countries a year 1 °C hotter lowers growth by about 1.3 percentage points. Rich countries show no robust effect.

Area weights give the temperature of the land. Population weights give the temperature where people live. The figure is panel A of Figure 1, “Changes and Variability in Temperature and Precipitation”, from the published version on MIT’s economics page.

Population weights in P

Population weights change only the entries of P. Each cell’s fraction is multiplied by the people who live in the cell.

  • Monterey County, California, averaged 28.3 °C in July 2012 by area and 20.5 °C by people. Tompkins County moves little.
  • project() moves the people onto the temperature grid. method = "sum" keeps their number.
R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; objects from part 3; reads data_clean/pop_2010.tif
popg <- project(rast("data_clean/pop_2010.tif"), tmax, method = "sum")  # people in each cell
tabN$wp <- tabN$coverage_fraction * values(popg)[tabN$cell]             # people in the part inside
tabN$wp <- tabN$wp / ave(tabN$wp, tabN$county, FUN = sum)               # sum to 1 in each county
Pp  <- sparseMatrix(i = tabN$county, j = tabN$cell, x = tabN$wp, dims = dim(P))
cdp <- Pp %*% M
k <- match(c("36109", "06053"), low$GEOID)      # Tompkins, New York and Monterey, California
data.frame(county = low$NAME[k], by_area = round(rowMeans(cd[k, ]), 1),
           by_people = round(rowMeans(cdp[k, ]), 1))        # July means, degrees C
#>     county by_area by_people
#> 1 Tompkins    28.3      28.8
#> 2 Monterey    28.3      20.5

tabN is the table of cells and fractions for the 3,109 counties in low. M is the 31 days as a matrix. The counts are the Gridded Population of the World for 2010.

The weight follows the question. Health and labour outcomes call for people, crops for cropland, and area answers a question about land. Clock: part 4 should start by minute 58.

4 · An application to heat and corn yields

Overview of part 4

Whether the counties with more heat lost more corn yield in 2012.

  • The event: the heat wave and drought of 2012 in the Corn Belt, the Midwestern states that grow most US corn.
  • Heat: degrees above 29 °C in each cell, added over July.
  • Cropland: the share of each cell under crops, as the weight.
  • The comparison: county heat against the change in corn yield, 1,391 counties.

Later in this part: each county’s heat against its 2012 corn yield.

The 2012 heat wave and corn yields

In 2012 corn yields fell across the Corn Belt. Illinois’s median county harvested 41% less per acre than its 2002 to 2011 mean.

  • I ask whether the counties with more July heat over their cropland lost more yield.
  • I use PRISM’s temperatures, a cropland grid from satellites and USDA county corn yields.

USDA is the US Department of Agriculture. A bushel of corn weighs 25.4 kg and an acre is 0.4 hectare. The map shows the 1,508 counties with a 2012 yield and at least 8 earlier years.

Heat above 29 °C in each cell

Heat is each day’s degrees above 29 °C, added over the 31 days of July 2012.

  • A degree day is one degree above the threshold for one day. A day at 35.8 °C counts 6.8, and a day at 28 °C counts 0.
  • Warren Hall’s cell has 48.3 degree days.
  • The largest total, 555, is in Death Valley, California.

29 °C is corn’s threshold in Schlenker & Roberts (PNAS, 2009).

Degree days from the 31 layers

(tmax - 29) * (tmax > 29) is each day’s degrees above 29 °C. sum() adds the 31 days in each cell.

  • (tmax > 29) is 1 on days above 29 °C and 0 on the others, so cooler days add nothing.
  • The heat runs from 0 to 555 degree days across the lower 48 states.
R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; tmax (July's 31 layers) and wh (Warren Hall) from part 1
heat <- sum((tmax - 29) * (tmax > 29))          # each day's degrees above 29, summed over 31 days
round(range(values(heat), na.rm = TRUE))
#> [1]   0 555
round(unlist(extract(heat, wh, ID = FALSE)), 1) # Warren Hall
#>  sum 
#> 48.3

On a stack, max() returns one layer, the largest value across the 31 days, so the heat is written as a sum.

Cropland on the temperature grid

The cropland grid has 3 km cells and the temperature grid 4 km cells. project() with method = "average" gives each temperature cell the mean cropland share over it.

  • Each cropland cell holds the percent of its land under crops, from 0 to 100.
  • Warren Hall’s cell is 4.5% cropland. The cell at the centre of Story County, Iowa, is 86.6%.
R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; tmax and wh from part 1; reads data_clean/cropland_2011.tif
crops <- rast("data_clean/cropland_2011.tif")   # percent of each cell under crops, 0 to 100
res(crops)
#> [1] 0.025 0.025
cropg <- project(crops, tmax, method = "average")   # the average share over each temperature cell
round(unlist(extract(cropg, wh, ID = FALSE)), 1)    # Warren Hall's cell, percent under crops
#> cropland 
#>      4.5

The University of Maryland’s GLAD laboratory classified satellite images of 2008 to 2011 (Potapov et al., Nature Food, 2022). A share is averaged when it moves to larger cells, and a count would be summed.

County heat over cropland

Each weight is now the cell’s fraction in the county times its cropland share. In part 3 it was the fraction alone.

  • Champaign, Illinois: 151 degree days by either weight.
  • Sequatchie, Tennessee: 105 over cropland, 78 over the county.
R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; tabN, P and low from part 3; heat and cropg from the slides before
tabN$wc <- tabN$coverage_fraction * values(cropg)[tabN$cell]   # fraction times cropland share
tabN$wc <- tabN$wc / ave(tabN$wc, tabN$county, FUN = sum)      # 0/0 in a county with no cropland
Pc <- sparseMatrix(i = tabN$county, j = tabN$cell, x = tabN$wc, dims = dim(P))
low$heat      <- as.vector(Pc %*% values(heat))                # heat over each county's cropland
low$heat_area <- as.vector(P %*% values(heat))                 # heat over the whole county
k <- match(c("17019", "47153"), low$GEOID)                     # Champaign, IL and Sequatchie, TN
data.frame(county = low$NAME[k], over_cropland = round(low$heat[k], 1),
           whole_county = round(low$heat_area[k], 1))
#>       county over_cropland whole_county
#> 1  Champaign         151.0        150.8
#> 2 Sequatchie         105.2         77.6

tabN is the table of every county’s cells and fractions. P is the matrix of area weights, and low is the 3,109 counties.

Hotter counties lost more corn yield

Counties with more heat over their cropland lost more of their corn yield. Across 1,391 counties the correlation is -0.42.

  • Each dot is a county east of the 100th meridian with a 2012 yield, at least 8 earlier years and some cropland.
  • The fitted line falls 2.2 percentage points for every 10 degree days.
  • Missouri’s median county had 213 degree days and lost 49%. Minnesota’s had 67 and gained 7%.

Drought struck the same counties in 2012, so the slope mixes heat and drought. West of the 100th meridian much of the corn is irrigated.

5 · An application to the Eaton fire

Overview of part 5

How many people lived downwind of the Eaton fire.

  • The picture: a satellite image of Los Angeles on 9 January 2025.
  • The wind: its speed and direction at the fire, from two layers.
  • Downwind: the population cells within 100 km in the wind’s direction.
  • The count: the people who live in those cells.

Later in this part: the downwind cells drawn over the smoke.

The Eaton fire on 9 January 2025

The Eaton fire started on the evening of 7 January 2025 in the hills north of Los Angeles. Two days later NASA’s Terra satellite photographed its smoke over the city.

  • I ask which way the wind blew at the fire that morning, and how many people lived downwind.
  • The picture is a raster with four layers. plotRGB() draws the first three as red, green and blue.
R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; reads data/nasa/worldview_terra_20250109.tif
img  <- rast("data/nasa/worldview_terra_20250109.tif")         # NASA's Terra, 9 January 2025
dim(img)                                                       # rows, columns, layers
#> [1]  966 1200    4
fire <- vect(cbind(lon = -118.069155, lat = 34.203483), crs = "EPSG:4326")   # the fire's origin
plotRGB(img); points(fire, pch = 21, bg = "red", col = "white", cex = 1.6)

The red dot is where the fire started, from CAL FIRE, California’s state fire agency. The plume at the coast, to the west, is the Palisades fire. NASA Worldview, MODIS on Terra.

The wind at the fire at 10 am

At 10 am on 9 January the wind at the fire blew at 9.7 km/h. It came from the north-east (36°) and blew toward the south-west (216°).

  • The two layers and the formulas are those used at Warren Hall. 18 UTC is 10 am in Los Angeles in January.
  • u = -1.6 means 1.6 m/s toward the west. v = -2.2 means 2.2 m/s toward the south.
R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; fire from the slide before; reads the two wind files in data_clean/
u_la <- rast("data_clean/u10_la_20250109.tif")                 # wind toward the east, by hour
v_la <- rast("data_clean/v10_la_20250109.tif")                 # wind toward the north
uf <- extract(u_la[["18:00"]], fire, ID = FALSE)[1, 1]         # 18:00 UTC is 10 am in Los Angeles
vf <- extract(v_la[["18:00"]], fire, ID = FALSE)[1, 1]
to <- (atan2(uf, vf) * 180 / pi) %% 360                        # the direction the wind blows toward
round(c(u = uf, v = vf, kmh = sqrt(uf^2 + vf^2) * 3.6,
        from = (atan2(-uf, -vf) * 180 / pi) %% 360, toward = to), 1)
#>      u      v    kmh   from toward 
#>   -1.6   -2.2    9.7   36.5  216.5

A wind cell here is about 23 km by 28 km. It averages the mountains and the basin, so it smooths the gusts in the canyons.

Overnight on 8 January ERA5 gives 19 to 23 km/h from 22° to 30°, far below the gusts that stations recorded. At 17:00 UTC (9 am) the wind is 10.2 km/h from 29°, toward 209°.

The cells downwind of the fire

A cell is downwind when it lies within 100 km of the fire and within 20° of the wind’s direction. 194 of the 2,166 cells in the picture qualify, 73 on land.

  • Each cell has a distance from the fire and a bearing, its direction from the fire clockwise from north.
  • I chose 100 km and 20°. Practice task 12 changes both.
R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; img, fire and to from the slides before; reads data_clean/pop_2010.tif
people <- crop(rast("data_clean/pop_2010.tif"), img)           # people per cell, 2010
km    <- values(distance(people, fire))[, 1] / 1000            # km from the fire to each cell
xy    <- xyFromCell(people, 1:ncell(people))                   # each cell's centre: lon, lat
east  <- (xy[, 1] - crds(fire)[1]) * cos(crds(fire)[2] * pi / 180)   # degrees east, shrunk at 34 N
north <-  xy[, 2] - crds(fire)[2]                              # degrees north of the fire
bear  <- (atan2(east, north) * 180 / pi) %% 360                # direction from the fire to the cell
gap   <- abs((bear - to + 180) %% 360 - 180)                   # degrees away from the wind
down  <- km <= 100 & gap <= 20
c(cells = ncell(people), downwind = sum(down))
#>    cells downwind 
#>     2166      194

At latitude 34° a degree of longitude is 0.83 of a degree of latitude. The code multiplies the east-west difference by the cosine of the latitude.

The gap line measures the angle the short way round, so 350° and 10° are 20° apart.

3.6 million people lived downwind

3.6 million people lived in the downwind cells in 2010, of the 16.7 million in the picture. That is 22%.

  • The yellow outline holds the 194 downwind cells. It covers the Eaton fire’s smoke from the foothills to Santa Catalina Island.
  • The population grid counts people per cell, so the answer is a sum. Cells over the ocean are NA.
R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; people and down from the slide before
n <- values(people)[, 1]
round(c(downwind = sum(n[down], na.rm = TRUE), in_the_picture = sum(n, na.rm = TRUE)))
#>       downwind in_the_picture 
#>        3620330       16661720
round(100 * sum(n[down], na.rm = TRUE) / sum(n, na.rm = TRUE), 1)
#> [1] 21.7

The outline comes from the wind and the fire’s location alone, and the picture was taken about 40 minutes before the wind’s hour. Smoke can pass high overhead, so ground monitors measure the air people breathed.
