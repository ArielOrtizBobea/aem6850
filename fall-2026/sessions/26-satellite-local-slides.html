Passive and active sensors

Sensors on satellites work in two ways.

  • A passive sensor records energy from the ground, such as reflected sunlight or heat. A camera is a passive sensor.
  • An active sensor sends its own signal and times what comes back. Radar and LiDAR are active sensors.
  • Radar works at night and through cloud.
  • Today we work with cameras.

Definitions from NASA Earthdata on remote sensing.

Cameras on drones, planes and satellites

Cameras fly on drones, planes and satellites. A pixel is one cell of an image.

  • A drone photographed one corn field in North Carolina at 4.1 cm.
  • A plane photographs the lower 48 states at least every 3 years for USDA’s aerial photos (NAIP), at 0.6 m.
  • The Sentinel-2 satellites photograph almost all the world’s land about every 5 days, at 10 to 60 m.

Drone: NC State Extension via OpenAerialMap, CC BY 4.0. Photo: USGS, USDA, The National Map. Contains modified Copernicus Sentinel data (2024).

Cayuga Lake on a cloudy day

On 30 August 2024 the camera saw almost only cloud over Cayuga Lake. Two days later, radar showed the lake’s outline.

  • Sentinel-2 carries a camera. The European Space Agency (ESA) marks about 99% of the land and water around the lake as cloud that day.
  • Sentinel-1 carries a radar. Its radio waves pass through cloud.
  • Calm water reflects the radar signal away from the satellite, so the lakes look dark.

Contains modified Copernicus Sentinel data (2024). Terrain-corrected radar from Microsoft’s Planetary Computer.

What a band records

A camera records light in separate bands.

  • A band is one range of wavelengths. The image stores one layer per band.
  • Wavelength is measured in nanometres (nm), billionths of a metre. Our eyes see about 400 to 700 nm.
  • Sentinel-2 adds near-infrared and shortwave infrared, which our eyes cannot see.
  • Other sensors record heat the ground gives off, or lights from towns after dark.

B8A is Sentinel-2’s narrow near-infrared band. Band centres from ESA.

Reading the drone image in R

In R, a drone image is a raster with three layers: red, green and blue.

  • 12.2 of the 47.1 million pixels (26%) are black corners outside the flight.
  • The file does not mark these pixels as missing. They have 0 in every layer.
  • mask() sets every pixel with 0 in all three layers to NA. Averages then skip these pixels.

Reading the image in R

Our image is a raster of 20 m cells with six layers. Five are bands, and the sixth is ESA’s class for each cell.

  • The file stores reflectance times 10,000 as whole numbers. Dividing by 10,000 gives reflectance.
  • Reflectance is the share of sunlight a surface sends back, from 0 to 1.
  • The bands are B02 blue, B03 green, B04 red, B8A near-infrared and B11 shortwave infrared.
  • SCL is ESA’s class for each cell. 4 is vegetation, 5 is not vegetated (bare ground, roofs and roads), and 6 is water.
R Console, in the 26-satellite-local folder; reads data/sentinel2/s2_20240815_cayuga_20m.tif (37 MB)
s2 <- rast("data/sentinel2/s2_20240815_cayuga_20m.tif")
s2
#> size        : 2916, 1490, 6  (nrow, ncol, nlyr)
#> resolution  : 20, 20  (x, y)
#> coord. ref. : WGS 84 / UTM zone 18N (EPSG:32618)
#> names       :   B02,   B03,   B04,   B8A,   B11, SCL
refl <- s2[[1:5]] / 10000                        # reflectance, from 0 to 1

SCL is short for scene classification layer. EPSG:32618 is UTM zone 18N, in metres. I averaged the 10 m bands to 20 m, so all six layers share one raster.

Reflectance of plants, bare ground and water

On 15 August 2024, plants in our image reflect 0.40 of near-infrared and only 0.03 of red. Water reflects almost no shortwave infrared.

  • zonal() takes the median of each band within ESA’s classes 4, 5 and 6.
  • Water’s median in shortwave infrared is 0.010. Water absorbs that light, so we expect a value near 0.
R Console, in the 26-satellite-local folder; s2 and refl from Reading the image in R
m <- zonal(refl, s2$SCL, fun = "median", na.rm = TRUE)   # about 6 seconds
m[m$SCL >= 4 & m$SCL <= 6, ]
#>   SCL    B02    B03    B04    B8A    B11
#> 2   4 0.0434 0.0602 0.0344 0.4034 0.1840

#> 4   6 0.0533 0.0535 0.0319 0.0473 0.0100

NDVI, a vegetation index

NDVI compares near-infrared with red. Green plants reflect much near-infrared and little red, so their NDVI is high.

  • NDVI is the normalized difference vegetation index.
  • It is near-infrared minus red, divided by their sum. It runs from −1 to 1.
R Console, in the 26-satellite-local folder; refl from Reading the image in R
ndvi <- (refl$B8A - refl$B04) / (refl$B8A + refl$B04)
names(ndvi) <- "NDVI"                              # otherwise the layer keeps the name B8A
plot(ndvi, col = hcl.colors(50, "Greens", rev = TRUE), plg = list(title = "NDVI"))

Cayuga Lake is the pale band down the middle of our image. Contains modified Copernicus Sentinel data (2024).

Lights at night and GDP growth

Henderson, Storeygard & Weil used lights at night to measure GDP growth where national accounts are weak.

  • Question: Henderson, Storeygard & Weil (American Economic Review, 2012) asked whether lights seen from space can measure GDP growth.
  • Method: They averaged the lights over each country’s land each year. They compared each country with itself over time.
  • Data: Lights from US Air Force weather satellites, 1992 to 2008, for 188 countries, in cells about 1 km wide.
  • Result: A 1% rise in lights goes with a 0.28% rise in GDP.

Figure 2, “Long-Term Growth: Korean Peninsula”, lights in 1992 (left) and 2008 (right). South Korea’s lights rose 72% and North Korea’s fell 7.4%.

Lights and population in New York’s counties

We sum the lights in each of New York’s 62 counties in 2023. Counties with more people give off more light, and the correlation of the logs is 0.87.

  • I used the newer VIIRS satellites, which need no account. The paper used the older Air Force satellites.
  • extract(..., fun = sum) adds up the light of the cells inside each county.
R Console, in the 26-satellite-local folder; reads data/lights/ and data_clean/census/
viirs <- rast("data/lights/ntl_viirs_2023_ny.tif")             # mean light at night, 2023
ny    <- vect("data_clean/census/ny_counties.gpkg")            # 62 counties, 2023 population
s     <- extract(viirs, ny, fun = sum, na.rm = TRUE, exact = TRUE)  # exact: share of each cell inside
cor(log(s[, 2]), log(ny$pop2023))                              # column 2 holds the sums
#> [1] 0.8724901

World Bank Light Every Night, World Bank Open Database License. Population from the Census Bureau.

Urban sprawl from land-cover maps

Burchfield and coauthors measured US sprawl from 30 m land-cover maps. Sprawl barely changed from 1976 to 1992.

  • Question: Burchfield, Overman, Puga & Turner (Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2006) asked how much new US development is scattered.
  • Method: Around each 30 m cell of housing, they took the share of open land in the square kilometre. More open land means more scattered building.
  • Data: Land use from aerial photos around 1976, and from Landsat in 1992.
  • Result: Open land averaged 42% in 1976 and 43% in 1992.

A land-cover map gives each cell a type of cover, such as forest, crops, water or built.

Figure IIa, “Urban Land in Atlanta, GA (Top Panel) and Boston, MA (Bottom Panel)”. Yellow is urban land around 1976, and red is land built from 1976 to 1992.

Crop output from NDVI

Asher & Novosad had no crop data for Indian villages, so they measured crop output with NDVI. New roads raised it by 1.7%, which is not significant.

  • Question: Asher & Novosad (American Economic Review, 2020) asked whether new rural roads in India raised farm output and village incomes.
  • Method: For each village, they took the season’s peak NDVI, the greenness index, minus its early-season value.
  • Data: Images from MODIS, a NASA sensor, in 250 m cells, 2000 to 2014.
  • Result: They rule out rises above 6.8%.

The paper has no such figure. I drew this one from our 2024 Sentinel-2 images to show the method. It is the median NDVI of cropland and forest near Aurora, New York.

NDVI of cropland and forest in August

On 15 August, cropland and forest around the lake have almost the same median NDVI. One date cannot tell crops from trees.

  • WorldCover is ESA’s free 10 m land-cover map of 2021 (CC BY 4.0). It codes trees 10, crops 40 and water 80.
  • Asher & Novosad subtract the early-season NDVI for this reason.

Crop burning in Punjab

Jack and coauthors paid farmers in Punjab not to burn rice straw. A classifier measured burning on each field.

  • Question: Do payments to farmers reduce crop burning? Jack, Jayachandran, Kala & Pande (American Economic Review: Insights, 2025) asked this in India.
  • Method: Monitors checked 681 fields for burning. A classifier learned from them and scored every field from images.
  • Data: 1,668 farmers in Punjab in 2019. Images at 3 m and 10 m.
  • Result: Paying part up front raised the share who did not burn by 10 percentage points.

A classifier puts each field into a class, here burned or unburned, from its band values.

Types of resolution

NASA lists four types of resolution. Today we use the first three.

  • Spatial resolution is the pixel size, 4.1 cm for the corn-field drone.
  • Temporal resolution is the time between images. Sentinel-2 passes over the lake about every 5 days.
  • Spectral resolution is the number and width of bands. The drone has 3 and Sentinel-2 has 13.
  • Radiometric resolution is the number of values a pixel can take. One series of lights at night stores only 0 to 63.

NASA Earthdata on remote sensing. NASA defines temporal resolution by the orbit. Here it is the time between images.

Measurement error in two reviews

Two reviews of satellite data in economics warn that numbers from images carry error.

  • Measurement error is the gap between our number and the true number.
  • Donaldson & Storeygard (Journal of Economic Perspectives, 2016) expect “false positives and false negatives” from any classification (p. 192).
  • They add that a 1 km cell of lights at night records light from 3 to 5 km around.
  • Jain (Review of Environmental Economics and Policy, 2020) names misclassification, saturation, the atmosphere and cloud.

Lights at night in 2023 around Ithaca, from the VIIRS satellites. The glow runs out over Cayuga Lake, where nobody lives. World Bank Light Every Night.

Saturation means the sensor stops counting at its top value. Jain’s list is from her abstract.

Forest loss in Mexico

A satellite map misses about half of Mexico’s forest loss. Modelling that error almost triples the payments’ effect.

  • Question: Mexico pays landowners to keep forest. Did the payments cut forest loss? Alix-García & Millimet (Journal of the Association of Environmental and Resource Economists, 2023).
  • Method: They modelled missed loss and false loss.
  • Data: 20,919 parcels, 2001 to 2014, and a 30 m Landsat map.
  • Result: The usual estimate cuts the yearly chance of loss by 0.5 points, not significant. With the errors modelled, the cut is 1.4 points.

Cayuga Lake’s area from ESA’s map

ESA’s map gives Cayuga Lake 42,944 acres. The state publishes 42,956.

  • The lake is the largest water body in our image. Each 10 m cell covers 100 m².
  • I multiplied the lake’s 1,737,902 cells by 100 m² and converted to acres. One acre is 4,046.86 m².

On the image’s 20 m cells ESA’s map gives 42,964 acres, and on its own cells 42,998.

Our image stops just past the lake’s north end and cuts the outlet canal. Counting the canal north of the edge gives 42,985 acres, 0.07% over the state’s figure.

Two water indices

MNDWI puts the lake at 42,655 acres, 0.7% under the state’s 42,956. NDWI puts it at 40,177 acres, 6.5% under.

  • NDWI, a water index, is green (B03) minus near-infrared (B8A), divided by their sum.
  • MNDWI, the modified version, uses shortwave infrared (B11) in place of near-infrared.
  • A cell is water where the index is above 0. I fixed this threshold before measuring.
R Console, in the 26-satellite-local folder; refl from Reading the image in R
mndwi <- (refl$B03 - refl$B11) / (refl$B03 + refl$B11)
ndwi  <- (refl$B03 - refl$B8A) / (refl$B03 + refl$B8A)
fm <- freq(patches(mndwi > 0, directions = 8, zeroAsNA = TRUE))
fn <- freq(patches(ndwi > 0, directions = 8, zeroAsNA = TRUE))
round(c(mndwi = max(fm$count), ndwi = max(fn$count)) * 400 / 4046.86)   # acres; a 20 m cell is 400 m2
#> mndwi  ndwi 
#> 42655 40177
  • NDWI misses about a quarter of the lake at its south end. The water there reflects about as much near-infrared as green.

200 points labelled on aerial photos

I labelled 200 points on aerial photos from 2019, 50 in each of four classes. A decision tree will learn the classes from them.

  • The classes are water, forest, grass or crops, and built.
  • The photos have 0.6 m pixels. A label is the class I read in the 20 m cell of our image at a point.
  • I skipped cells that held two classes or that I could not call.

a, the 200 points over our image of Cayuga Lake. b, two examples of each class. Photos from USGS, USDA, The National Map. The labels are in data_clean/labels/labels_200.csv.

Training points and test points

We set 60 of the 200 labelled points aside as test points. The decision tree learns from the other 140 and never sees the 60.

  • We first read the five bands of our image at each point.
  • set.seed() fixes the draw, so the same 60 come out every time.
R Console, in the 26-satellite-local folder; reads data_clean/labels/labels_200.csv; s2 and refl from part 3
lab <- read.csv("data_clean/labels/labels_200.csv")             # lon, lat, class
lab$class <- factor(lab$class)
pts <- project(vect(lab, geom = c("lon", "lat"), crs = "EPSG:4326"), crs(s2))
lab <- cbind(lab, extract(refl, pts, ID = FALSE))               # five bands per point
set.seed(6850)
test  <- sample(nrow(lab), 60)
train <- lab[-test, ]                                           # 140 points to learn from
hold  <- lab[test, ]                                            # 60 test points
table(train$class)
#> 
#>          built         forest grass or crops          water 
#>             33             37             34             36

A decision tree on our labels

A decision tree sorts each point by yes/no questions about its five bands. Ours first splits at a near-infrared (B8A) value of about 0.068.

  • At each split, the points that meet the condition go left.
  • The 36 training points below 0.068 are all water.
  • Among the rest, red (B04) of about 0.059 or more means built.
R Console, in the 26-satellite-local folder; train from Training points and test points
fit <- rpart(class ~ B02 + B03 + B04 + B8A + B11, data = train, method = "class")   # the answer is a class
par(mar = c(0, 0, 0, 0))                     # no blank margin around the tree
plot(fit, uniform = TRUE, margin = 0.08)     # uniform: every level of the tree as tall
text(fit, cex = 0.9)

Accuracy and the confusion table

The tree gets 52 of the 60 test points right (87%). The confusion table shows which classes it mixes up.

  • Accuracy is the share of test points the model gets right. Naming the largest class (built) for every point would score 28%.
  • In a confusion table the rows are the true class and the columns the tree’s class. The diagonal holds the right answers.
  • The tree called 4 of the 13 forest points grass or crops.
R Console, in the 26-satellite-local folder; fit and hold from the slides before
pred <- predict(fit, hold, type = "class")

Torchiana, Rosenbaum, Scott & Souza-Rodrigues (Review of Economics and Statistics, 2025) show that small map errors inflate measured change. In Mato Grosso, Brazil, a map 92% accurate showed 6.2% of cropland turning to pasture each year, against a true 0.7%.

Applied to every cell, the tree calls 12.0% of the image built, against ESA’s 1.6%. A quarter of my labels are built, so a rare class looks common. The map and its crosstab are on the page.

Summary

A camera records light in bands, and R turns the bands into indices, areas and maps. Every number from an image carries error.

  • NDVI was 0.84 for plants and 0.19 for water in our image of Cayuga Lake.
  • Papers used lights at night to measure GDP growth, and land-cover maps for sprawl.
  • Only 5 of the 406 water bodies at 10 m were left at 250 m.
  • ESA’s map put the lake at 42,944 acres. The state publishes 42,956.
  • Our decision tree got 52 of 60 test points right (87%).
