25 · Radar & LiDAR

Tuesday, Nov 24, 2026

Slides · Live script · Source · the project

In this session we measure elevation, heights and hail with active sensors: radar and LiDAR. An active sensor sends its own signal and measures what comes back. We start with radar. We read a radar elevation raster of Tompkins County and subtract USGS’s ground elevation from it. We also map radar hail in seven Plains states and compare it with hail reports, from my own preliminary work. Then we read the USGS LiDAR data for Cornell’s central campus with the lidR package. From these points we build three rasters with 1 m cells: the ground elevation, the surface elevation, and the height of buildings and trees. We compare our heights for McGraw Tower and the Empire State Building with their published heights. We also see how a thin tower loses height in larger cells. Last, we use USGS’s elevation raster of Tompkins County, with 30 m cells. From it we compute slope, the view from McGraw Tower and the land that drains to Fall Creek. Each use comes with a published study.

The project. Download the zip on this page, unzip it and open 25-radar-lidar.Rproj. R then starts in the project folder, and every path on the slides is relative to it. The scripts install lidR, terra, sf and exactextractr if they are missing. I downloaded every file before class, so you do not need an account, a key or the network. The folder’s README.md says where each file came from and under what licence. The zip leaves out the raw downloads: the original LiDAR tiles, the Manhattan points, the USGS elevation tile, the radar tile and the index of USGS projects. tools/ holds the code that downloaded them, and data_clean/ holds the files I made from them.

Active and passive sensors

Sensors on planes and satellites work in two ways.

  • A passive sensor records energy from the ground, such as reflected sunlight. A camera is a passive sensor.
  • An active sensor sends its own signal and times what comes back. Radar and LiDAR are active sensors.

Source: NASA Earthdata on active and passive instruments.

Radar and LiDAR measure distance, so they give elevation and height. Radar also works at night.

NASA Earthdata defines the two types this way: “Active instruments emit energy and collect data based on changes in the return signal”, and passive instruments “detect energy emitted or reflected from an object”. Its page on active instruments says LiDAR and radar find the distance to a target by timing the pulse that comes back. Its page on radar says radar gives images “night or day, regardless of weather conditions”.

The main active sensors

Every active sensor sends a signal and records the echo.

Sensor What it measures
Weather radar (radio waves) rain, hail and wind

LiDAR (laser light) elevation, heights of buildings and trees
Sonar (sound in water) depth of oceans and lakes
Ground-penetrating radar (radio waves) pipes and objects under the ground

Soil conductivity sensors (electric field) clay, water and salt in farm soil
Sodar (sound in air) wind from 30 m to 600 m up

Radar, LiDAR and sonar give free maps of large areas. Today we zoom in on these three.

More on each sensor, with its source:

  • Weather radar. NOAA’s radars measure rain and hail, and the Doppler shift of the echo gives the wind. NCEI keeps the archive free (NCEI).
  • Radar on satellites. Altimeters time a pulse sent straight down to get the height of the sea surface (NASA). Scatterometers send pulses at an angle and get the wind over the ocean (JPL).
  • Ground-penetrating radar. EPA lists finding pipes, tanks and other utilities, archaeology, and unmarked graves (EPA). Each survey covers one site.
  • Seismic surveys. Air guns and other sources send sound into the sea floor. The echoes show sediment and rock from metres to kilometres down (USGS). Data collected by companies stay confidential for 25 years (BOEM). USGS and BOEM give free access to more than 1,100 released surveys (NAMSS).
  • Soil conductivity sensors. Farmers and researchers tow them across a field. The reading depends on clay, water and salt, to about 1.5 m deep (USDA ARS). I found no public archive of these surveys.
  • Sodar. It sends sound up and gets wind speed and direction from about 30 m to 600 m. The example is a sodar at Argonne National Laboratory (DOE Data Explorer).

I found no economics paper in a top journal that uses data from the last four sensors.

How radar and LiDAR work

Radar and LiDAR both send a signal and measure how long it takes to come back.

  • Radar sends radio waves. Radio waves pass through clouds (NASA).
  • LiDAR sends laser light. Clouds block it, so the plane flies under the clouds (NOAA).

A longer time means the surface is farther away.

NASA’s page says radar gives images “night or day, regardless of weather conditions”. NOAA’s primer says that, unlike radar, lidar cannot see through clouds, rain or dense haze. Radar waves are a few centimetres long. LiDAR light is about a micrometre long.

How LiDAR measures distance

LiDAR stands for light detection and ranging.

  • A plane flies over an area and sends laser pulses to the ground.
  • The sensor times each pulse. A longer time means a surface farther away.
  • A pulse can come back more than once, first from a treetop and then from the ground.

A US Navy survey plane that carries a LiDAR for coastal mapping, 2013. US Navy photo by Lt. Cmdr. Corey Barker, public domain (Wikimedia Commons).

Each time a pulse comes back is called a return. GPS and a motion sensor on the plane give each return its x, y and elevation.

In the drawing, the treetop sends back the first return and the ground the last. The ground is 30 m below the treetop. The flying height of 1,500 m in the drawing is an example.

How sonar measures depth

Sonar works like radar. It sends sound through water and times the echo.

  • Light and radio waves fade fast under water. Sound travels about 1,500 m per second.
  • A multibeam sonar sends a fan of beams. Each pass maps a strip of the floor.
  • Only 28.7% of the ocean floor is mapped in detail. Satellites estimate the rest from bumps in the sea surface.

The sources are NOAA’s pages on sonar and survey ships, and GEBCO’s 2026 release.

Sonar stands for sound navigation and ranging. The depth comes from the time between the pulse and its echo. NOAA’s tutorial on nautical charts says sound travels farther than light or radar under water, at about 1,500 m per second. An echo from 100 m down comes back in about 0.13 seconds (2 × 100 / 1,500).

  • History. Fessenden’s oscillator echoed off an iceberg and the sea bottom in April 1914. USS Stewart sounded across the Atlantic with an echo sounder in
    1. The first multibeam sonar went on a Navy ship in 1963 (NOAA Ocean Exploration timeline).
  • Detail falls with depth. NOAA’s page on multibeam sonar says “the deeper the water depth, the lower resolution this map”.
  • Shallow water. Green LiDAR from planes also maps the floor where the water is shallow and clear. NOAA says the depth it reaches depends on how clear the water is (Digital Coast).
  • Satellites. Mountains on the sea floor pull the sea surface into bumps, and radar satellites measure them. NOAA says this is “not as accurate or detailed as echo sounding from ships” (NOAA STAR).
  • Free data. The GEBCO grid covers the world’s oceans in 15 arc-second cells and is in the public domain (GEBCO).
  • Cayuga Lake. I found no depth raster for the whole lake. USGS mapped the Cayuga Inlet channel in Ithaca in July 2016 with a single-beam echo sounder (USGS).

Port depth and container ports

Sonar measures how deep a port is. Depth predicted which US ports took container ships.

  • Question: Brooks, Gendron-Carrier & Rua (Journal of Urban Economics, 2021) asked whether container ports made nearby counties grow.
  • Method: Container ships need deep ports. They used each port’s depth in 1953, before containers, to predict which ports switched.
  • Data: Each port’s depth in 1953, in eight bands, from the World Port Index. The population of 3,023 US counties from 1950 to 2010.
  • Result: Counties near container ports grew an extra 70% between 1950 and 2010.

Green counties are near a container port in 2010 and near a port 30 feet deep or more in 1953. Blue counties are near a container port only. Figure 3b of the 2018 working paper (Federal Reserve, FEDS 2018-045).

The authors call a port “very deep” when it was 30 feet deep or more in 1953. Ships before containers did not need more. The World Port Index records depth in eight categories. Its deepest category was “40 feet and above” in 1953 and “76 feet and over” in 2015. Darker colours on the map are closer to a port, in bands of 0 to 100, 100 to 200 and 200 to 300 km.

The 70% comes from the abstract of the 2018 working paper. The figure is from the same open copy.

The paper does not use sonar data or a depth raster. It uses the depth band that the 1953 World Port Index gives for each port’s wharf. The results hold with the anchorage and channel depths, which the Index also reports (footnote 17 of the working paper). US survey ships measured depth with echo sounders from the 1920s (NOAA). I have not found how the Index measured its 1953 depths.

When sonar, radar and LiDAR appeared

Sonar came first, in the 1910s, and radar in the 1930s. LiDAR needed the laser, which was invented in 1960.

Each date comes from the agency’s or the paper’s own page. The list is on the session page.

LiDAR is now in cars and in phones.

The dates and their sources:

  • 1914. Fessenden’s oscillator echoes off an iceberg and the sea bottom (NOAA Ocean Exploration).
  • 1922. USS Stewart sounds across the Atlantic with an echo sounder. Within five years nearly all Coast Survey ships carry one (NOAA Ocean Exploration).
  • 1963. The first multibeam sonar goes on the Navy ship Compass Island (NOAA Ocean Exploration).
  • 1935. Watson-Watt and Wilkins detect an aircraft with radio waves at Daventry, England (Royal Society).
  • 1942. The Navy gives the Weather Bureau 25 surplus aircraft radars (National Weather Service).
  • 1950. The first airport surveillance radars (ASR-1) are deployed (FAA).
  • 1951. Carl Wiley at Goodyear conceives synthetic aperture radar (Lockheed Martin).
  • 1952. Washington National Airport starts radar departure control (FAA).
  • 1960. Maiman reports the first laser, made of ruby (Maiman, Nature, 1960).
  • 1963. A laser “optical radar” probes the upper atmosphere (Fiocco and Smullin, Nature, 1963).
  • 1971. A laser altimeter on Apollo 15 measures the Moon (NASA).
  • 1978. Seasat carries a radar into orbit (JPL).
  • 1999. A journal issue is devoted to laser mapping from planes (Wehr and Lohr, ISPRS Journal of Photogrammetry and Remote Sensing, 1999).
  • 2000. The Space Shuttle maps the elevation of most of the world’s land (USGS).
  • 2005. Stanley, a self-driving car, wins the DARPA Grand Challenge (Thrun et al., Journal of Field Robotics, 2006).
  • 2014. Europe launches the radar satellite Sentinel-1A (ESA).
  • 2016. USGS has its first full year of LiDAR mapping for 3DEP (USGS).
  • 2018. NASA launches ICESat-2 and GEDI (ICESat-2, GEDI).
  • 2020. Apple puts a LiDAR scanner in the iPhone 12 Pro (Apple).
  • 2024. NHTSA requires automatic emergency braking in new cars by 2029. Its cost estimate assumes a radar sensor (Federal Register).

What sets the detail of a sensor

A beam spreads as it travels, so a sensor sees less detail from farther away.

  • At twice the distance, the spot is twice as wide.
  • Laser light spreads very little. From a plane, a LiDAR spot is 30 to 50 cm wide.
  • More power lets a sensor hear an echo from farther away. It does not narrow the beam.

The radar beam is 0.925° wide (NOAA). A NOAA ship’s sonar beams are 1° wide. I computed the widths. Angles are drawn wider than they are.

The widths are 2 × distance × tan(angle / 2). The radar beam of 0.925° is 1.6 km wide at 100 km and 3.2 km wide at 200 km. A sonar beam of 1° is 1.7 m wide at 100 m deep and 70 m wide at 4,000 m. The sonar drawing is not to scale in depth.

  • Weather radar. NOAA’s radars focus their energy into a beam of 0.925°. The data are recorded every half degree, with cells 250 m long along the beam (NOAA ROC). The length comes from how long each pulse lasts, so it does not grow with distance.
  • Sonar. NOAA Ship Okeanos Explorer carries a Kongsberg EM 304 sonar. Its beams are 0.5° along the ship and 1° across, in a fan of 150° with 512 beams (NOAA, 2024 readiness report). Sound is slow, so the sonar waits for each echo before the next ping. Deep water gets fewer pings per hour.
  • Radar on satellites. It combines echoes taken along its path, as if it had a much larger antenna (NASA Earthdata). That is how radar from orbit gives detail that one dish could not.
  • LiDAR. NOAA’s guide gives 50,000 to 150,000 pulses a second, spots of 30 to 50 cm on the ground, and points 1 to 2 m apart or closer (NOAA, Lidar 101). The number of points per m² also depends on how fast and how high the plane flies and on the overlap between flight lines.
  • Policy. The free Copernicus elevation data come in 30 m and 90 m cells. They are derived from the WorldDEM radar model. A 10 m version over Europe is open only to public authorities, Copernicus services, EU institutions and EU-funded projects (Copernicus).

Free LiDAR data from USGS

The US Geological Survey (USGS) publishes free LiDAR data for almost the whole country.

  • USGS collected the data one project at a time.
  • USGS’s elevation program is called 3DEP. It reports that 99% of the country is available or in progress (USGS).
  • USGS requires at least 2 points per m². This standard is called quality level 2 (USGS, Lidar Base Specification).
  • USGS mapped Alaska with radar, because clouds cover it so often (USGS).

You can download the data from LidarExplorer, The National Map, or Amazon’s cloud without an account. The data are US government work and in the public domain (AWS Open Data).

USGS gives the 99% “at the end of fiscal year 2025”. A fiscal year is the US government’s budget year. USGS lists the quality levels on its quality-levels page. Quality level 2 (QL2) means at least 2 points per m², 10 cm vertical accuracy and a 1 m elevation raster. QL1 and QL0 have at least 8 points per m².

LiDAR data outside the US

Several other countries publish free LiDAR data for the whole country.

  • England covered the whole country from 2017 to 2023 (Environment Agency).
  • The Netherlands has flown the country four times. The latest flight has about 10 to 14 points per m² (AHN).
  • Switzerland has covered the whole country since 2024 (swisstopo).
  • Spain has flown the country three times since 2009 (IGN, PNOA-LiDAR).
  • GEDI is a NASA LiDAR on the International Space Station. It measures the height of forests in spots 25 m wide, along tracks 600 m apart.

GEDI samples the land between 51.6° N and 51.6° S (GEDI). Most of the ground between its tracks is never measured. The Dutch data are called AHN.

England’s data come with a 1 m raster of the whole country. Swiss flights reach 15 to 40 points per m² in the newest areas. Other national programmes and space lasers:

  • France. IGN’s LiDAR HD had published 47.8% of the country by December 2025, with 10 or more points per m² on average. IGN plans to publish the rest (except French Guiana) by the end of 2026 (IGN).
  • Estonia. The whole country is covered. Estonia publishes ground, surface and canopy rasters made from flights of 2021 to 2024 (Land and Spatial Development Board).
  • New Zealand. A national programme brought coverage to over 80%. The 1 m ground and surface rasters are free to download (LINZ).
  • GEDI. Its spots are 25 m wide and 60 m apart along each track. The eight tracks are 600 m apart, and each laser fires 242 times a second. NASA also grids the shots into 1 km cells (GEDI). GEDI’s pages give no revisit schedule. The data start in April 2019, with a pause from March 2023 to April 2024.
  • ICESat-2. NASA launched this laser satellite in 2018. It measures spots 11 m wide up to 88° north and south (NASA).
  • Global rasters. Elsewhere, researchers use canopy-height rasters built from GEDI and ICESat-2. They also use radar elevation rasters such as SRTM and Copernicus GLO-30.

Radar elevation minus ground elevation

Radar elevation includes trees and buildings. On half of Tompkins County it sits more than 5 m above the ground.

  • USGS publishes the elevation of the ground, without trees or buildings.
  • Both rasters have 30 m cells on the same grid. We subtract them cell by cell.

Ground elevation

rasterize_terrain() builds a raster of ground elevation from the ground points.

  • Each cell is 1 m by 1 m.
  • Under a building there are no ground points. R fills those cells from the ground points around the building.
  • This raster is called a digital terrain model (DTM).

tin() joins nearby ground points into triangles and reads the elevation off them. The ring marks McGraw Tower.

R Console, in the 25-radar-lidar folder; las, the campus points without noise
ground <- rasterize_terrain(las, res = 1, algorithm = tin())   # bare ground, 1 m cells

The ground elevation goes from 205.3 m at the foot of Libe Slope to 264.3 m above sea level. TIN stands for triangulated irregular network. Many sources call a DTM a DEM, a digital elevation model. The call took about 4 seconds.

Surface elevation

rasterize_canopy() builds a raster of the elevation of the top of buildings and trees.

3,278 cells have no point, mostly on dark roofs where no pulse came back. na.fill = tin() fills them with triangles, as for the ground.

Steep land and housing supply

  • Question: Saiz (Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2010) asked why some US cities build few new homes when prices rise.
  • Method: He measured the share of land within 50 km of each city centre that is water or steeper than 15%.
  • Data: He used a USGS elevation raster with 90 m cells, satellite maps of water and wetlands, and US metro areas.
  • Result: Most metro areas that build few new homes when prices rise are surrounded by water and steep slopes.

Inelastic supply means few new homes get built when prices rise. The figure is Figure 1.D of the 2008 working paper, “Embedding LA Data into 50km Radius”. Red is steeper than 15%, and the blue ring is 50 km around Los Angeles.

Slope with terrain()

terrain() computes the slope of each cell from its eight neighbours.

  • Slope is rise over run. A slope of 15% means 15 m of rise over 100 m, or 8.5 degrees.
  • terrain() returns the slope as an angle. tan() turns it into rise over run, and multiplying by 100 gives percent.
  • The red cells on the map are steeper than 15%.
R Console, in the 25-radar-lidar folder; dem from the slide before
slope_pct <- tan(terrain(dem, v = "slope", unit = "radians")) * 100   # percent
steep     <- slope_pct > 15                                         # TRUE where steeper than 15%

Slope from 4 neighbours and from 8. terrain() uses all eight neighbours by default. With neighbors = 4 it uses only the cells north, south, east and west. On this raster the county’s share of land steeper than 15% barely changes:

R Console, in the 25-radar-lidar folder; dem, towns and slope_pct from above

              four  = sum(exact_extract((s4 > 15) * 900, towns, "sum", progress = FALSE))) /
        sum(towns$ALAND), 1)
#> eight  four 
#>  16.7  17.1

Computing slope by hand

To see what terrain() does, I computed one slope by hand.

  • I used the 3 × 3 window around McGraw Tower’s cell. I got 6.235%, as terrain() does.
  • For the rise to the east, I subtract the west column from the east column. The middle row counts twice.
  • The weights add to 4 and the columns are 60 m apart. So I divide by 4 × 60 = 8 × 30 m.

The rise from north to south works the same way, and the slope combines the two. This weighting is Horn’s formula, which terra uses.

The slope by hand in R

Six lines of R give the same slope as terrain() at the tower’s cell.

  • tw is McGraw Tower in the raster’s coordinates.
  • w is the 3 × 3 window of elevations around the tower’s cell.
  • dx and dy are the rise per metre to the east and to the north.
R Console, in the 25-radar-lidar folder; dem and slope_pct from the slides before
tw <- project(vect(cbind(-76.48505, 42.44757), crs = "EPSG:4326"), crs(dem))   # McGraw Tower
rc <- rowColFromCell(dem, cellFromXY(dem, crds(tw)))                          # row 753, col 651
w  <- matrix(values(dem, row = rc[1] - 1, nrows = 3, col = rc[2] - 1, ncols = 3), 3, byrow = TRUE)
dx <- (sum(w[, 3] * c(1, 2, 1)) - sum(w[, 1] * c(1, 2, 1))) / (8 * 30)
dy <- (sum(w[3, ] * c(1, 2, 1)) - sum(w[1, ] * c(1, 2, 1))) / (8 * 30)
round(c(by_hand = 100 * sqrt(dx^2 + dy^2), terrain = extract(slope_pct, tw)[1, 2]), 3)
#> by_hand terrain 
#>   6.235   6.235

Wind turbines in view of homes

  • Question: Gibbons (Journal of Environmental Economics and Management, 2015) asked what a view of a wind farm costs the people nearby.
  • Method: He computed the viewshed of each wind farm on an elevation raster with 90 m cells. Then he compared changes in house prices where turbines came into view.
  • Data: Home sales in England and Wales from 2000 to early 2012, and 148 wind farms in operation.
  • Result: House prices fell 5 to 6% where a wind farm came into view within 2 km.

A viewshed is the land you can see from a point. A hedonic study uses house prices to measure what people pay for a view, a park or clean air. Light grey is land that sees at least one turbine tip, and dark dots are the postcodes of homes. Figure 3 of the accepted version (LSE Research Online), one wind farm in north east England.

Computing the view in R

viewshed() returns TRUE for each cell we can see from the tower.

  • tw is McGraw Tower. viewshed() draws a line of sight from it to every cell.

  • Each cell is 30 × 30 = 900 m². land drops the lake, whose surface is flat at 115.95 m.

Terrain as an instrument

Three papers used terrain as an instrument.

  • An instrument changes who gets a treatment. It affects the outcome only through that treatment.
  • Duflo & Pande (Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2007) used river gradient, which predicts where India built dams.
  • Olken (American Economic Journal: Applied Economics, 2009) used mountains that block television signals to villages in Indonesia.
  • Yanagizawa-Drott (Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2014) used hills that blocked a hate radio station during Rwanda’s 1994 genocide.

My drawing, from the Tompkins raster east of McGraw Tower. From the top of the tower, a ridge 5.2 km east at 438 m hides the land behind it.

The profile: the eye at 53 m sees the ground out to 2.43 km, loses it behind a bump to 3.96 km, sees it again to 5.22 km, and then the ridge hides everything beyond. Results, each from its abstract. Duflo and Pande: poverty fell in downstream districts and rose in the district where the dam was built. Olken: better reception went with less participation in village groups and lower trust. Yanagizawa-Drott: about 10% of the violence, about 51,000 perpetrators, is attributed to the station.

River pollution near sewage plants

In this section we find the land that drains to a point on a creek. We start with a study of river pollution, which needed to know what lies upstream.

  • Question: Keiser & Shapiro (Quarterly Journal of Economics, 2019) asked whether federal grants to city sewage plants made US rivers cleaner. They also asked whether the benefits covered the cost.
  • Method: They compared pollution downstream and upstream of each plant, before and after its grants. They used a model of the US river network.
  • Data: 50 million readings from 240,000 monitoring sites, 1962 to 2001, and 35,000 grants to treatment plants.
  • Result: Making one mile of river fishable for a year cost about $1.5 million. This is more than the gain in home values.

The Clean Water Act (1972) is the main US federal law on water pollution. The map is panel A of Figure I of the 2018 working-paper version (Cowles Foundation), “National Maps of Water Pollution Data”.

Which way water flows

Water in each cell flows to its steepest downhill neighbour. If we count the cells that drain through each cell, the streams appear.

Panel a is a real 3 × 3 window of elevations on Fall Creek, a stream through Ithaca. Its middle cell drains south, to its lowest neighbour. Panel b shows the count for every cell on a log scale. The dark lines are streams.

Flow direction and accumulation in R

Two calls compute flow direction and flow accumulation from the 30 m elevation raster around Fall Creek.

  • Flow direction is the neighbour each cell drains to.
  • Flow accumulation is the number of cells that drain through each cell.
  • A pit is a cell lower than all its neighbours, where water stops. I filled the 10,000 or so pits in the raster before class.
R Console, in the 25-radar-lidar folder; reads data_clean/terrain/fallcreek_dem_filled.tif
filled <- rast("data_clean/terrain/fallcreek_dem_filled.tif")   # 30 m, pits filled before class
fd  <- terrain(filled, v = "flowdir")                          # the neighbour each cell drains to
acc <- flowAccumulation(fd)                                    # cells draining through each cell

The fill took about 4 minutes, and the filled raster is in the project. The two calls above take under a second.

Quick reference

Function What it does
rast() reads a raster file, such as an elevation raster
compareGeom() TRUE when two rasters have the same cells
mask() sets cells to NA outside polygons, or where another raster has a given value
readLAS() reads a LAS or LAZ file of LiDAR points
filter_poi() keeps the points that meet a condition
rasterize_terrain(), tin() builds the ground raster from ground points, through triangles
rasterize_canopy(), p2r() builds the surface raster from the highest point in each cell
buffer() a circle (or band) of a given distance around a point, line or polygon
aggregate() merges blocks of cells into one larger cell
terrain() computes slope, aspect or flow direction from an elevation raster
viewshed() the cells visible from one point
ifel() a value where a condition holds and another where it does not
as.polygons(), disagg() cells into polygons; one polygon into its separate pieces
expanse() the area of each polygon
flowAccumulation() how many cells drain through each cell
watershed() the cells that drain to one point (terra’s)
