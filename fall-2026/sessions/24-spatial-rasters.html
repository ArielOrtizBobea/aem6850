24 · Spatial II: rasters

Thursday, November 19, 2026

Slides · Live script · Source · the project

A raster stores data as a grid of equal cells, and each cell holds one number. This session reads PRISM’s daily maximum temperature for July 2012 into R with terra. It finds the cell over Warren Hall, crops the grid to New York and computes new grids cell by cell. It moves wind from its own grid onto the temperature grid and turns the wind’s two layers into a speed and a direction. It then averages the cells over each county: first with a packaged function, then as a weight matrix times the matrix of cells by days. The weights can be area, people or cropland. Two applications close the session: July heat over cropland against the 2012 corn yield loss, and the people who lived downwind of the Eaton fire on 9 January 2025.

The project. Download the zip on this page, unzip it and open 24-spatial-rasters.Rproj, so that R starts in the project folder. Every path on the slides is relative to it. The scripts install terra, sf, exactextractr and Matrix if they are missing. I downloaded every file before class, so nobody needs an account, a key or the network. The folder’s README.md says where each file came from and under what licence. The raw downloads stay out of the zip: 30 of PRISM’s 31 daily files, the ERA5 wind files, the cropland grid, the population grid and the corn yields. tools/ holds the code that fetched them, and the small files made from them are in data_clean/.

Objectives

By the end of this session you can:

  • Read a grid with rast() and read its printout: size, resolution, extent, CRS and the range of values.
  • Explain that a raster is a matrix of cells by layers, find a place’s cell and read its value on every day.
  • Crop and mask a grid to a state, and compute new grids cell by cell: differences, counts of days, degree days.
  • Move one grid onto another grid’s cells with project(), and compute wind speed and direction from u and v.
  • Compute county means of a grid with a packaged function, and build the same means from the table of cells and fractions, as a weight matrix P times the matrix of cells by days.
  • Change the weights (area, people, cropland) by changing the entries of P.

Left out: elevation and slope, night lights and vegetation measured from satellites, turning weather-station records into a grid.

Cells, resolution and extent

A raster is a grid of equal cells. Each cell holds one number.

  • A cell (or pixel) is one square of the grid.
  • The resolution is the size of a cell: 1/24 of a degree, about 3.4 km by 4.6 km at Ithaca.
  • The extent is the rectangle the grid covers: here the lower 48 states.

PRISM’s daily maximum temperature, from Oregon State University. Warren Hall is a Cornell building in Ithaca, in Tompkins County, New York.

One layer per day

One file can hold many layers on the same cells. tmax_july2012.tif holds 31, one for each day of July 2012.

  • A layer is one grid. A stack is several layers on the same cells.
  • Each layer is named by its date. PRISM’s day ends at 8 am in New York. The layer dated 18 July holds the afternoon of 17 July.
  • values() of the stack is a matrix of cells by days.
R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; i from the slide before; reads data_clean/tmax_july2012.tif

#> [1] "2012-07-01" "2012-07-02" "2012-07-31"
range(time(tmax))                               # the dates, stored as dates
#> [1] "2012-07-01" "2012-07-31"
dim(values(tmax))                               # cells by days
#> [1] 872505     31
values(tmax)[i, 16:18]                          # Warren Hall's cell on 16, 17 and 18 July
#> 2012-07-16 2012-07-17 2012-07-18

I built tmax_july2012.tif from PRISM’s 31 daily files with tools/make_clean.R. PRISM’s day. PRISM’s documentation (PRISM datasets) defines a day as the 24 hours from 12:00 UTC on the day before to 12:00 UTC on the date of the file. A daily maximum is reached in the afternoon, so it falls in the file dated the next day. The wind of 18:00 UTC on 17 July falls in the temperature layer dated 18 July.

Cropping and masking to New York

crop() keeps the rectangle of cells around New York. mask() then sets the cells outside the state to NA.

  • The Census Bureau’s county file has 3,235 counties, 62 of them in New York. The counties and the grid share one CRS, NAD83.
  • The crop has 109 rows by 190 columns, and it renumbers the cells. Warren Hall’s cell is now 11,669.
R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; tmax from the slide before; reads data/census/cb_2024_us_county_500k.zip
unzip("data/census/cb_2024_us_county_500k.zip", exdir = "data_clean/counties")
co  <- st_read("data_clean/counties/cb_2024_us_county_500k.shp", quiet = TRUE)   # county outlines
ny  <- co[co$STUSPS == "NY", ]                  # New York's 62 counties
st_crs(ny) == st_crs(tmax)                      # do the counties and the grid share one CRS?
#> [1] TRUE
tny <- crop(tmax, ny)                           # the cells inside New York's bounding box

#> [1] 109 190  31

#> [1] 11669

Daily temperature at Warren Hall

extract() reads a point’s cell in every layer. Warren Hall’s hottest layer is the one dated 18 July, at 35.8 °C.

  • project() puts the point, built from longitude and latitude, in the grid’s CRS.
  • 13 of the 31 layers pass 30 °C. The month’s mean is 29.9 °C.
  • The same three lines serve any list of points, one row per point.
R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; tny from the slide before
wh <- vect(cbind(lon = -76.4797, lat = 42.4490), crs = "EPSG:4326")   # Warren Hall: longitude first
wh <- project(wh, crs(tny))                     # the point in the grid's coordinate system
wh_t <- unlist(extract(tny, wh, ID = FALSE))    # the value of Warren Hall's cell in each layer
round(wh_t[16:19], 1)
#> 2012-07-16 2012-07-17 2012-07-18 2012-07-19 
#>       28.6       31.0       35.8       29.7

EPSG:4326 is the CRS of GPS coordinates. Web maps show latitude first, and vect() takes longitude first.

Arithmetic cell by cell

  • Question: Miguel, Satyanath & Sergenti (Journal of Political Economy, 2004) asked whether a fall in income causes civil war in Africa.
  • Method: Rain drives farm income. They averaged gridded rainfall over each country and year, then took the change from the year before. That change is their instrument for income growth.
  • Data: rainfall from satellites and rain gauges on a 2.5-degree grid, for 41 African countries, 1981 to 1999.
  • Result: A fall of five percentage points in growth raised the chance of civil conflict the next year by one half.

An instrument changes the treatment (here, income growth) and affects the outcome (civil war) only through it. The figure is Figure 1 of the paper, “Current economic growth rate on current rainfall growth”, from the open copy on Miguel’s Berkeley page.

17 July minus July’s mean

Arithmetic on rasters works cell by cell. In the 17 July layer, New York’s cells ran from 1.2 °C below their July mean to 4.6 °C above.

R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; tny, ny and wh from part 1
t17 <- tny[["2012-07-17"]]
dev <- t17 - mean(tny)                          # mean() of a stack averages its 31 layers
round(range(values(dev), na.rm = TRUE), 1)
#> [1] -1.2  4.6
g <- c(t17, mean(tny), dev); names(g) <- c("day", "month_mean", "difference")   # c() stacks layers
round(unlist(extract(g, wh, ID = FALSE)), 1)    # Warren Hall
#>        day month_mean difference 
#>       31.0       29.9        1.0
plot(g, nc = 3, fun = function() lines(vect(ny)))

tny is July’s 31 days cropped to New York and wh is Warren Hall’s point.

If useful: the difference map shows round spots around weather stations. PRISM estimates each cell from stations.

Counting days in each cell

  • Question: Schlenker & Roberts (PNAS, 2009) asked how temperature affects US corn, soybean and cotton yields.
  • Method: They built daily temperatures on a 4 km grid. In each cell they counted the time spent in each 1 °C interval, then averaged each county’s cropland cells.
  • Data: county yields, 1950 to 2005, east of the 100th meridian for corn and soybeans.
  • Result: Corn yields rise with temperature up to 29 °C and fall steeply above it. The threshold is 30 °C for soybeans and 32 °C for cotton.

A yield is the harvest per unit of land. The 100th meridian runs through the Great Plains.

Days above 30 °C in New York

tny > 30 is TRUE or FALSE in every cell and day, and sum() counts the TRUE days. New York’s cells had 0 to 26 days above 30 °C.

  • TRUE counts as 1 and FALSE as 0. sum() of a stack adds its layers in each cell and returns one layer.
  • Warren Hall’s cell gives 13, the count from its 31 values.
  • The most days fall along the Hudson valley north of Albany and in New York City. The fewest are in the Adirondacks.
R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; tny, ny and wh from part 1
hot <- sum(tny > 30)                            # TRUE or FALSE per cell and day; sum() counts TRUE

#> [1]  0 26
unlist(extract(hot, wh, ID = FALSE))            # Warren Hall
#> sum 
#>  13
plot(mask(hot, ny)); lines(vect(ny))

tny is July’s 31 days cropped to New York. 30 °C is a round number for this example.

Wind and temperature on different grids

To use wind and temperature in the same cell, both must be on the same grid.

  • Question: what was the wind in Warren Hall’s temperature cell at 2 pm on 17 July 2012?
  • Temperature comes from PRISM on 4 km cells.
  • Wind comes from ERA5 on 0.25-degree cells. One wind cell covers 36 temperature cells.

ERA5 is the European weather centre’s model run over past observations. A wind cell is about 21 by 28 km at Ithaca.

Moving the wind onto the temperature grid

project() gives every temperature cell the wind of the cells around it. The wind in Warren Hall’s cell barely changes: 4.78 m/s before, 4.73 after.

R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; tny and wh from part 1; reads data_clean/u10_ny_20120717.tif
u <- rast("data_clean/u10_ny_20120717.tif")     # wind toward the east, m/s, one layer per hour
res(u); res(tny)                                # cell size in degrees
#> [1] 0.25 0.25
#> [1] 0.04166667 0.04166667
compareGeom(u, tny, stopOnError = FALSE)        # do the two grids have the same cells?
#> [1] FALSE
u4 <- project(u[["18:00"]], tny)                # weighted mean of nearby wind cells, in tny's CRS
compareGeom(u4, tny, stopOnError = FALSE)
#> [1] TRUE
round(c(before = extract(u[["18:00"]], project(wh, crs(u)), ID = FALSE)[1, 1],
        after = extract(u4, wh, ID = FALSE)[1, 1]), 2)        # Warren Hall, m/s
#> before  after 
#>   4.78   4.73

compareGeom() is TRUE when two grids have the same cells. 18 UTC is 2 pm at Ithaca in July.

Wind speed and direction

  • Question: Deryugina, Heutel, Miller, Molitor & Reif (American Economic Review, 2019) asked how fine particles affect deaths among the elderly.
  • Method: They computed the wind direction at each pollution monitor from the eastward and northward wind. Then they averaged each county’s monitors by day. The direction brings pollution from elsewhere, so it is their instrument.
  • Data: wind on a 32 km grid, and Medicare records by county and day, 1999 to 2013.
  • Result: One more microgram per cubic metre for one day caused 0.69 more deaths per million people over 65 within three days.

Medicare is US public health insurance for people 65 and over. Fine particles (PM2.5) are smaller than 2.5 micrometres. The figure is Figure 2, from PubMed Central. Its title is “Relationship between Daily Average Wind Direction and PM2.5 Concentrations for Counties in and around the Bay Area, CA”.

The wind at Warren Hall

County averages of gridded weather

  • Question: Deschênes & Greenstone (American Economic Review, 2007) asked how climate change would affect US farm profits.
  • Method: For rainfall they laid a land-use map over PRISM’s 4 km cells and averaged the farmland cells of each county. Temperature came from weather stations. Then they compared each county’s profits in its hotter and cooler years.
  • Data: the Census of Agriculture of 1987 to 2002, PRISM for rainfall, and about 8,000 stations for temperature.
  • Result: Predicted warming would raise yearly farm profits by about $1.3 billion, or 4%.

Heat above 29 °C in each cell

Heat is each day’s degrees above 29 °C, added over the 31 days of July 2012.

  • A degree day is one degree above the threshold for one day. A day at 35.8 °C counts 6.8, and a day at 28 °C counts 0.
  • Warren Hall’s cell has 48.3 degree days.
  • The largest total, 555, is in Death Valley, California.

29 °C is corn’s threshold in Schlenker & Roberts (PNAS, 2009).

Degree days from the 31 layers

(tmax - 29) * (tmax > 29) is each day’s degrees above 29 °C. sum() adds the 31 days in each cell.

  • (tmax > 29) is 1 on days above 29 °C and 0 on the others, so cooler days add nothing.
  • The heat runs from 0 to 555 degree days across the lower 48 states.

The cells downwind of the fire

A cell is downwind when it lies within 100 km of the fire and within 20° of the wind’s direction. 194 of the 2,166 cells in the picture qualify, 73 on land.

  • Each cell has a distance from the fire and a bearing, its direction from the fire clockwise from north.
  • I chose 100 km and 20°. Practice task 12 changes both.
R Console, in the 24-spatial-rasters folder; img, fire and to from the slides before; reads data_clean/pop_2010.tif

north <-  xy[, 2] - crds(fire)[2]                              # degrees north of the fire

#>    cells downwind 
#>     2166      194

At latitude 34° a degree of longitude is 0.83 of a degree of latitude. The code multiplies the east-west difference by the cosine of the latitude.

The gap line measures the angle the short way round, so 350° and 10° are 20° apart.

Quick reference

The pattern to reuse on any polygons and any stack on one grid:

  1. exact_extract(r[[1]], polygons, include_cell = TRUE) gives one table of cells and fractions per polygon.
  2. Bind the tables with a polygon index and drop the cells with no value.
  3. Divide each fraction by its polygon’s sum, so the weights sum to 1.
  4. sparseMatrix(i, j, x, dims = c(nrow(polygons), ncell(r))) builds P.
  5. P %*% values(r) gives polygons by layers.
  6. Compare one polygon with exact_extract(r, polygons, "mean").
  7. saveRDS(P) keeps P for the next stack on the same grid.
