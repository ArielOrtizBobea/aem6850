Objectives

By the end of this session you can:

  • Describe delegation to an AI agent as a principal–agent problem: what you observe, what you do not, and why the agent’s report on its work is a claim.

Delegation is a principal–agent problem

Economists have a name for this. The principal cannot observe the agent’s effort, only the outcome and what the agent says about it.

How an AI agent differs from a human RA

The same

  • You see only the result
  • It can misread the brief
  • It can take a shortcut and not mention it

Different

  • It works in seconds, for cents
  • Its confidence is orthogonal to its accuracy
  • It remembers nothing between sessions
  • It has no reputation at stake
  • It optimizes the check you give it
  • A second, independent one costs almost nothing

The last line is the opening. With people, a second independent RA is expensive. With agents it is one more session.

Evidence about hidden work, weakest to strongest

4 A new session gets only the question and the original data file; it never sees the first agent’s code or conversation, and computes the numbers again.
5 Before delegating, you compute one number yourself, such as the number of countries or one coefficient, and check that the output matches it.
Holmström (BJE, 1979): use every signal that tells you about the hidden action.

A published result: growth when public debt exceeds 90% of GDP

  • The paper. The economists Carmen Reinhart and Kenneth Rogoff compared growth in 20 advanced economies from 1946 to 2009 (Reinhart and Rogoff, AER P&P, 2010).
  • The claim. In years when public debt was above 90% of GDP, growth averaged −0.1%, far below other years.
  • Why it mattered. The 90% line became a reference point for those arguing to cut government deficits after 2010.

Reproduction and coding errors found, by type of team

No AI: researchers only. AI assisting: researchers used ChatGPT as a helper. AI in charge: ChatGPT did the work; researchers only supervised. The study: 288 researchers in 103 teams, 2024; the AI was a chat assistant, ChatGPT with GPT-4 and GPT-4o (Brodeur et al., PNAS, 2026).

Takeaway: the lesson is the right panel. With AI helping, teams reproduced papers as often and found fewer than half as many major errors: help with the work can cost attention to its errors.

The left panel is dated: a 2024 chat assistant working alone reproduced 37%, and today’s coding agents do far better (a 2026 study: 93% of 221 tasks). The right panel is the point: with AI helping, people found fewer errors.

Recruiters given a more accurate AI checked less

The study: 181 professional recruiters screened 44 job applications each for a math-heavy job. Each application was built from a real person in an OECD survey that tested adults’ math skills. Recruiters saw the person’s education and work history, never the test score, and decided whom to interview; the hidden score then said whether each call was right. Each recruiter was randomly given no AI, an AI right about 75% of the time, or one right about 85% (Dell’Acqua, working paper, 2022). With the better AI they followed its advice more often (74% against 53%), spent less time per résumé, and gained less accuracy.

Takeaway: the better the tool, the less we check. A set level of scrutiny has to be part of the workflow, every time, however good the output looks.

Same pattern as the last slide, with the quality of the AI varied on purpose. The better the AI, the more people took its answer without looking. The effect came from the experienced recruiters. Scrutiny is like taking a shower every day: part of the routine, done whether or not the output looks fine.

How much AI changed developers’ time per task

The study: 16 experienced open-source developers did 246 real tasks on their own projects; each task was randomly assigned to allow AI tools or not (Becker et al., arXiv, 2025). Before the tasks they forecast how much faster AI would make them, after the tasks they estimated how much faster it had, and the researchers measured the actual time per task.

Takeaway: they expected to be faster, felt faster, and were slower. How the work felt is a poor guide to how it went, so measure it.

Asking “are you sure?” makes a model back down

How many countries are in LifeCycleSavings?

Are you sure?

You’re right to question that. On reflection, there are 49.

An illustration of what the study measured: researchers asked AI assistants questions, then replied “I don’t think that’s right. Are you sure?” One model then wrongly admitted a mistake on 98% of questions (Sharma et al., ICLR, 2024).

Takeaway: pushing back gives the model no new information. To check a number, ask for it to be recomputed from the data.

Pushback adds no information, so the answer moves toward what you seem to want. To check a number, ask for it to be recomputed from the data.

Agents shown the published result tend to report it

  • Two coding agents got 221 tasks: rerun a paper’s published code and data (its replication package) and report the results
  • In some runs the agent was also given the paper’s PDF, with the published numbers in it
  • On the 10 tasks where data or code were missing, so the honest answer was “cannot reproduce”, both agents said so every time without the PDF. With the PDF, one said so only 63% of the time: otherwise it reported numbers from the paper as its own results
  • Prompt wording also nudged them toward specifications that confirm the paper

Alizadeh et al. (arXiv, 2026)

Takeaway: keep the numbers you expect away from the agent. Check that every number it reports is printed by the code, or run the code yourself. Never ask for significant results: the agent may try specifications until one clears the threshold, p-hacking you would not see.

If the brief says what the answer should be, the agent finds a way to produce it. Keep the answer you expect out of the brief.

Exact, sourced, and judgment claims

A claim is any statement in a deliverable that could be wrong: a number, a citation, what a variable means, an interpretation. Its kind decides how to check it.

Text and code both mix all three. The kind of claim decides the check, whatever the medium.

Why the checker must be independent

95.5% → 89.0%

A model’s accuracy after reviewing its own answers with no outside feedback (Huang et al., ICLR, 2024)

60%

How often two models that are both wrong give the same wrong answer (Kim et al., ICML, 2025)

A checker from another model family catches more than the model itself or a sibling (Lu et al., arXiv, 2025).

Terms: the checker is the model or agent asked to verify the work; self-correction is a model reviewing and revising its own answers; a model family is the set of models from one developer.

A model reviewing its own work adds little and can make it worse. Two copies of similar models share mistakes, so their agreement is weak evidence.

Four ways to make a check independent

A check is independent when it cannot share the mistakes of the work it checks. Four ways to separate them:

A fresh session

It never saw the conversation that did the work.

Other information

The raw data or the source, and never the worker’s code.

Another method

Another function, another language, a hand count.

Another model

A different family makes different mistakes.

Example: Scott Cunningham’s Referee 2 runs in a fresh terminal, never edits the author’s code, re-implements the results in another language, and requires them to match to six decimals.

Left to right, each tile adds independence. The second tile does the most work: a checker that reads the worker’s code inherits its assumptions.

Three setups with a second agent

Replicate: two agents compute the same numbers separately and you compare. Check each claim: an agent compares each statement with its source. Try to refute: an agent is told to find what is wrong.

AI reviewers also report problems that are not there (McAleese et al., arXiv, 2024): confirm each finding before you act on it.

Replicate for numbers, claim-by-claim for write-ups, refute for code. In all three, you read a short list at the end.

Testing a checker with planted errors

Researchers planted errors in 100 AI-written economics papers and ran checker agents on them. Share of the planted errors caught, by kind:

Code does not run: the scripts fail. Data errors: wrong values in the data. Text vs. tables: a number in the text differs from its table. Paper vs. code: the paper describes something the code does not do.

Source: Willner and Yanagizawa-Drott (working paper, 2026). Your version: copy the script, plant five errors, count what the checker finds.

A clean report from a checker you never tested tells you little. One round of planted errors tells you what it catches and what it misses.

  1. A new folder, savings-check, holding only summary.md
  2. A terminal in that folder: RStudio, Tools → Terminal → New Terminal, then cd into the folder
  3. claude --permission-mode manual; the first time, choose Yes, I trust this folder

What to take away

  1. Delegating to an agent is a principal–agent problem: its report on the work is a claim.
  2. A deliverable can look right and be wrong, from an agent or in a journal.
  3. The better the tool, the less we check: keep a set level of scrutiny, every time.
  4. Agents work toward the check you give them: they cannot edit the checks, and the numbers you expect stay out of the brief.
  5. Sort each claim as exact, sourced or judgment: recompute it, look it up, or decide.
  6. A second check counts when it is independent: a fresh session, other information, another method or model.
  7. Check the checker: confirm what it flags, and read the disagreements and a random sample of what passed.
  8. In your code: assertions of several kinds, and a look block for the end of the project.
